Kannappar is the hunter among the sixty-three Nayanmars. The Periya Puranam calls him Thinnan, the son of a hunter chief, and tells how, on the hill of Kalahasti, he came upon the Lord in the form of a lingam and could not leave him. He knew nothing of worship by the book. He roasted meat, tasted each piece to be sure it was good, and set the best on a leaf; he carried water for the Lord's bath in his own mouth, brought flowers in his hair, and cleared away the old flowers with his sandalled foot (Periya Puranam, Kannappa Nayanar Puranam 118–123). His companions thought him possessed and went home without him (119–120).

Each morning the temple priest, Sivagochariyar, found the shrine disturbed, cleaned it, and worshipped by the rules of the Agamas, grieving over what he found (152, 155). The Lord came to the priest in a dream and told him not to think of the hunter as a rough man: all his form was love, all his knowledge was knowledge of the Lord, and all his acts were dear. Let the priest hide, and he would see (156–158).

On the sixth day, as the hunter came up the hill, blood began to flow from one eye of the lingam (161, 164). Thinnan fell, rose, wiped it, searched the forest for healing herbs, and found that nothing helped (165–172). Then he remembered the saying that flesh is the remedy for flesh. He took out his own eye with an arrow and set it in place of the Lord's right eye, and when the bleeding stopped he danced for joy (172–175). When the other eye began to bleed, he set his foot on it to mark the place and lifted his arrow to his second eye. The Lord's hand rose and held his, and the Lord's voice said three times, "Stop, Kannappa" (176–178). The Lord took his hand and told him to stay always at his right side, while the gods rained flowers (179–180). From that day he is Kannappar, "the one who gave his eye".

The Thiruppugazh recalls him in three songs. In song 510, sung at Chidambaram, the hunter of the hills teaches true knowledge to the learned brahmin, Sivagochariyar, and the Lord delights in his leaves, his mouthful of water and the bow on his shoulder. Song 664 (Vellikaram) and song 1211 tell how the Lord gladly accepted the meat the hunter had first tasted himself, as if it were nectar. For Arunagiri, Kannappar is the measure of love without learning, which the Lord prizes above every rite.
