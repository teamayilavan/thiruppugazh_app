Two stories of Sundarar and gold lie behind this film, and they should be kept apart. The first is the one Arunagiri tells. In song 947, sung at Thirupukkoliyur (Avinashi), the commentary explains that Sundarar, wishing to delight his wife Paravai of Tiruvarur by showering her with a thousand pieces of gold, went to many shrines of the Lord to earn it; singing his Tevaram hymns there with steady rhythm and a determined, overflowing heart, he became exhausted by the heat and the walking. This telling was not found in the Periya Puranam, and is given here as the song tells it.

The second story is told by the Periya Puranam, in the life of Sundarar's friend, the Chera king Cheraman Perumal. Sundarar was returning from the Chera country with gifts, and as he passed through the forest near Tirumurugan Poondi in the Kongu country, a band of hunters fell on his party and carried off everything. They were not hunters at all, but the Lord's own hosts, sent by him (Periya Puranam, Kazharitrarivar Nayanar Puranam 164–168).

Sundarar went into the temple of Tirumurugan Poondi, and with melting love sang a hymn to the Lord, asking in it why he chose to live in such a wild place, where hunters rob travellers on the road (170). As he sang, by the Lord's grace, all the goods the hunters had taken were heaped up high before the temple gate, and Sundarar received them and went on to Tiruvarur (171–172). The story is told with a smile: the Lord plays with his friend, takes his treasure, and gives it back, for the sake of a song.

Arunagiri's own interest in the story is clear from the setting of song 947. He addresses Murugan as the Lord who appeared to him in person at Vayalur and took charge of him, and he recalls Sundarar's weary journeys for gold as the labour of a devotee whom the Lord loved. Whether the gold was earned shrine by shrine, as the song says, or taken and returned by the Lord's hosts, as the Puranam says, the gold in each case is a gift, and the walking and the singing are the devotion.
