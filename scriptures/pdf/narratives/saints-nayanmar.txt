The Nayanmar are the sixty-three saints of Tamil Shaivism whose lives Sekkizhar told in the Periya Puranam, the twelfth book of the Tirumurai. Arunagiri lived among their shrines and knew their stories, and his songs name some of them in passing, together with later poets of the same faith.

Appar, also called Thirunavukkarasar, was first known as Marulneekkiyar. The Periya Puranam tells that his sister Tilakavathiyar gave him holy ash when he was struck by a painful illness at Tiruvadigai, and that he returned to the worship of Shiva (Periya Puranam, Appar Puranam 67). The Pallava king, then devoted to the Jains, had him shut in a kiln of burning lime, which felt to him like a cool pool (95, 98); a maddened elephant was sent against him and turned away (119); and he was bound to a stone and cast into the sea, where he sang the hymn beginning "Sorrunai vedhiyan" and was carried safely to shore (126; Tevaram 4.11.1). He also worshipped at the hill of Tiruchirappalli (Appar Puranam 302).

Sundarar was claimed by the Lord on his wedding day, and his first hymn, "Pitha pirai soodi", calls the Lord a madman in loving reproach (Tevaram 7.1.1). The Periya Puranam tells how, when Sundarar's wife Paravai at Tiruvarur was angry that he had married Sangili at Tiruvorriyur, the Lord agreed to go to her on his behalf (Eyarkon Kalikkama Puranam 329), walked by night as Sundarar's messenger to her door (337, 340), and urged her to forget his faults and put aside her anger (344). Manikkavasagar, whose story belongs to the Tiruvilaiyadal Puranam rather than to the Periya Puranam, is remembered for the jackals of the forest that the Lord turned into horses for his sake. Kachchiyappa Sivachariyar of Kanchipuram is the author of the Kanda Puranam; tradition says that Murugan gave him its first line.

In the Thiruppugazh these figures appear in brief. Song 972, at Ilanji, speaks of the Lord who delighted in Sundarar's song and went as his messenger. Songs 960, at Madurai, and 1313, at Pazhamudhircholai, name Manikkavasagar of Vadavur and the jackals turned into horses. Song 1114 calls Murugan the guru who blessed Kachchiyappar. The name Appar appears in songs 555, 862 and 911; in some of these lines the word may simply mean "father", the Lord himself, though in song 555, at Tiruchirappalli, the poet speaks of the hill where Appar worshipped, which fits the saint.
