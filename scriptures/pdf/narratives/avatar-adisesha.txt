The picture of Vishnu lying on a great serpent in the ocean is less a story than an image, and it returns again and again in the Puranas, in the temples and in Tamil devotion. The Bhagavata Purana gives it its clearest form when it tells how the worlds begin. When the three worlds were submerged in the waters of dissolution, Vishnu lay alone on his bed, the great serpent Ananta; he seemed to sleep, yet his eyes were not wholly closed (Bhagavata Purana 3.8.10). All living beings rested within him, like fire held within wood, and he lay so for a thousand cycles of the four ages (3.8.11–12).

The same chapter shows the image again as Brahma saw it at the end of his long meditation: on the water a vast white bed, the body of the serpent, with the Lord lying on it alone, and the jewels on the serpent's hoods lighting up the darkness all around (3.8.23). The Bhagavata compares the Lord to a mountain crowned with golden peaks, the thousand golden-helmeted hoods of Ananta rising above him (3.8.29–30). From this resting Lord the lotus of creation grows, as the story of Brahma's birth tells.

Kamban returns to the image at the close of his Vamana story. Having given the worlds back to Indra, the Lord went back to his bed on the milk ocean of spreading waves, and the feet that had crossed all the worlds grew red under the hands of Lakshmi (Kamba Ramayanam, Bala Kandam, Velvi padalam 27). The Alvars sing it often. Andal recalls the Lord's rest on the serpent (Thiruppavai 6); Thirumangai Alvar sings of the generous Lord, like a rain cloud, reclining in the milk ocean (Periya Thirumozhi 8.9.2); and Poigai Alvar envies the dark ocean itself, because the Lord lies upon it (Mudhal Thiruvandhadhi 19).

Arunagiri recalls the image in twenty-six songs. He seldom builds a story around it; a single line names Murugan the nephew of the Lord who sleeps on the serpent, or on the sea. Song 11 (Thiruparankundram) speaks of the supreme one who sleeps on the serpent and once churned the ocean; song 134 (Palani) of the great Lord of Srirangam who rests on the serpent's hood; song 895 (Athippattu) of the pure green cloud asleep on the spotted serpent.
