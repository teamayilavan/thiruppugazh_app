Kumbhakarna, the younger brother of Ravana, was a giant of enormous size and strength who slept for months at a time. When the war had turned against Lanka, Ravana had him woken with great difficulty, with drums, conches and every kind of noise, and laid the situation before him. The widely told frame of the story, found in both Valmiki and Kamban, is that Kumbhakarna spoke plainly: his brother had done wrong in carrying off Sita and in ignoring good counsel. Yet he would not abandon Ravana in his danger, and went out to fight for him. That mixture of honesty and loyalty has made him one of the most sympathetic figures on the side of Lanka.

On the battlefield he drove the monkey army before him and even carried off Sugriva, who freed himself. At last Rama faced him. Valmiki ends the fight with Rama cutting off Kumbhakarna's head with an arrow, and the giant's fall striking the city of Lanka and its wall (Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha 67.165–172).

Kamban tells the final combat in stages. Kumbhakarna's right arm is cut off with the sword still in it, and the rakshasas tremble while the gods rejoice (Kamba Ramayanam, Kumbakarnan Vadhai Padalam 337); still he fights on, taking up a vast club (344). Then comes the most moving moment in Kamban's account. Kumbhakarna, whose face has been disfigured in the fight, asks Rama a favour: so that sages and gods may not look on him and say "a face without a nose", Rama should cut his neck with his arrow and send his great head into the dark sea (357). Rama grants it, and the head sinks into the ocean (359). In Kamban's hands the giant dies with dignity, asking only that his shame not be seen.

No Divya Prabandham verse narrating Kumbhakarna's death was found for this study; the Alvars mention him, if at all, only in passing.

Arunagiri names him once, in song 961 (Madurai). There Murugan is called the nephew who delights the heart of Mukunda, the Lord who with his bow destroyed Khara with Trishira, Kumbhakarna steadfast in his strength, and the ten-headed one who had conquered the gods. Arunagiri's single word for Kumbhakarna there, steadfast, fits the tradition's picture of him well.
