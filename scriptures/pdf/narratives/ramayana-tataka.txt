When the sage Vishwamitra came to Ayodhya and asked King Dasaratha for his young son Rama, to guard a sacrifice against the demons who kept defiling it, the king, after much anguish, let both Rama and Lakshmana go. On the way to Vishwamitra's hermitage the sage led the princes through a desolate forest once rich and populous, now laid waste by the demoness Tataka, mother of the demon Maricha.

Vishwamitra urged Rama to kill her for the sake of the people, telling him that a prince should not hold back from such a deed when it protects others. Rama accepted the sage's word, which his father had commanded him to obey. He sounded his bowstring, and Tataka came at the two princes in fury. In the fight that followed Rama's arrow struck her in the chest and she fell dead (Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda 26.25). The gods praised the deed, and Vishwamitra, well pleased, later bestowed celestial weapons on Rama.

Kamban sharpens the moment of the encounter. Tataka, he says, guessed the intent of the prince of the land of the Ganges and, herself like fire, hurled at him a trident of fierce flame together with the fire of her cruel eyes (Kamba Ramayanam, Tataka Vadhai Padalam 45). Rama loosed at her, whose colour was like night, a burning arrow as swift as a curse; it did not stay in her heart, hard as the stone of a diamond hill, but passed right through and out beyond (49). Rama's reluctance to strike a woman, which many retellings dwell on, belongs to the tradition's framing of the scene rather than to these verses.

The Tamil devotional poets remember it as the first of Rama's deeds. Kulasekhara Azhwar sings of the single arrow that brought down Tataka as she came against the sacrifice (Perumal Thirumozhi 10.2).

Arunagiri names Tataka in two songs, both times as the opening act of Rama's mission. In song 366, at Thiruvanaikka, he calls Murugan the nephew of the ocean of grace who killed Tataka when she came against the sacrifice hall in the forest hermitage of the sages who chant the Gayatri. In song 649, at Kathirgamam, he joins three deeds in one line: Rama loosed one arrow at the fury of Tataka, saw the sacrifice through, and broke the great bow. For Arunagiri the young prince's first arrow already shows the protector of the sages.
