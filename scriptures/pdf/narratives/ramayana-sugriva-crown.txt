After Vali fell by Rama's arrow, the kingdom of Kishkindha passed to his younger brother Sugriva. Valmiki describes Vali's funeral with royal honours, his son Angada lighting the pyre, and then devotes a whole chapter to Sugriva's coronation (Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 26). Hanuman invited Rama to enter the city and preside over the rite, but Rama declined, since he had vowed to spend the fourteen years of his exile in the forest and would not enter a town. The coronation was therefore performed by the monkey elders. Sugriva was consecrated with water brought in vessels of gold and seated as king, and Angada, Vali's son, was installed as heir apparent. Since the rainy season had begun, Rama asked Sugriva to wait in his city until the rains were over before beginning the search for Sita, and Rama himself withdrew to a mountain cave to wait.

Kamban gives the crowning a small but telling change. In his account it is Lakshmana, at Rama's order, who places the crown on Sugriva's head, in accordance with the rules laid down by those learned in the matter (Kamba Ramayanam, Arasiyal Padalam 5). The chapter's name, the chapter on kingship, reflects the counsel on the conduct of a king that surrounds the coronation in Kamban's telling.

For the Azhwars this moment completes the restoration of a dispossessed devotee. Periyazhwar, retelling the story in a verse on Srirangam, recalls how Rama made friends with Sugriva, killed Vali and restored to Sugriva both his wife and his kingdom (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 4.9.1).

The episode touches the Thiruppugazh in a single line, and that line is open to two readings. In song 128, at Palani, Arunagiri speaks of Raghava, of the Sun's lineage, who graciously gave the long crown and the kingdom to the younger brother, so that he might live in prosperity, after a fierce battle; Murugan is that Raghava's nephew. The younger brother who receives the crown may be Sugriva, younger brother of Vali, or Vibhishana, younger brother of Ravana; the words fit both. Either way, the line shows what Arunagiri valued in the story: the Lord who fights so that a younger brother who has taken refuge with him may be restored and given a crown.
