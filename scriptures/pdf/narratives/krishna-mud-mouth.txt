The Bhagavata Purana tells this story in a few verses of its tenth book, among the childhood scenes in Gokula. One day, while Krishna was playing with Balarama and the other cowherd boys, his friends came to Yashoda together and complained, "Mother, Krishna has eaten earth" (Bhagavata Purana 10.8.32). Yashoda, always anxious for his welfare, took him by the hand, meaning to look into his mouth and scold him, and asked why he had eaten dirt in a lonely place when all his friends, his elder brother among them, said so (10.8.33–34).

Krishna denied it. "Mother, I have never eaten earth," he said. "They are all lying. If you think they speak the truth, look into my mouth yourself." She told him to open his mouth, and he did (10.8.35–36). Inside it she saw the whole universe: everything moving and unmoving, the sky and its directions, the mountains, islands and oceans, the surface of the earth, the wind, fire, moon and stars, and with them Vraja and her own self (10.8.37–39).

The Bhagavata dwells on her bewilderment. She asks herself whether this is a dream, an illusion, a trick of her own mind, or some power belonging to her child (10.8.40), and she surrenders to the Lord who is beyond thought and speech, recognising that the sense of being Nanda's queen, Krishna's mother and the mistress of the cowherd village is itself his illusion (10.8.41–42). Then the Lord, by his own power, drew her back into a mother's affection. She forgot what she had seen and took her son on her lap as before, with even greater love; the Supreme Being whom the Vedas and Upanishads study, she thought of as her ordinary child (10.8.43–45). The point of the story, as the Bhagavata tells it, is not the vision alone but the love that is allowed to cover it.

No Alvar verse on this particular episode was found in the course of this research, although the Alvars often praise the Lord who holds the worlds within himself.

Arunagiri touches the theme in two songs. Song 674, sung at Thiruvalangadu, calls Murugan the nephew of the one who swallowed the world and who sheltered the cows under a hill held up like an umbrella. Song 1170 speaks of the wondrous Lord who ate the earth and played the flute. The phrase "who ate the earth" can also recall the Lord who holds the worlds in his stomach at the end of an age; the songs do not tell the story of the mud and the mother, but tradition links these lines to it.
