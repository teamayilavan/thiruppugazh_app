At the end of the long war in Lanka, when Rama and Ravana had fought from their chariots and neither had been able to bring the other down, Rama's charioteer Matali, sent by Indra, reminded him of the weapon he held in reserve. It was the weapon of Brahma, which Rama had received earlier in his journey and which carried the power of the Creator himself (Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda 108).

Rama took up the arrow, consecrated it with the sacred words prescribed for it, fitted it to his bow and drew the string. When he released it, the arrow struck Ravana in the chest and tore through his heart; the great bow fell from Ravana's hands, and he fell lifeless from his chariot (Yuddha 108.15–22). The gods, who had watched the battle, let fall a rain of flowers, and the long fear of the worlds was at an end.

Kamban gives the moment a cosmic scale. The arrow, he says, went forth bearing the root mantra of the four-faced Brahma, holding within it wind and fire whose end could not be seen; its blaze passed beyond the mountains that ring the world and beyond the great sea, so that the sun of the final deluge seemed a firefly beside it. In that instant, joined with the power of the discus, Brahma's weapon entered the chest of the wicked one, and the earth, every quarter and the sky itself reeled (Kamba Ramayanam, Ravana Vadhai Padalam 194–196).

The Tamil devotional tradition remembers the scene as the culmination of Rama's mission. Periyazhwar sings of the Lord who first stripped Ravana of his weapons and sent him back, and then destroyed him (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 4.8.5); Kulasekhara Azhwar recalls Ravana's end after the bridge was built (Perumal Thirumozhi 10.7); and Andal praises the one who went to southern Lanka and destroyed it (Tiruppavai 24).

Arunagiri recalls Ravana's death in at least nine songs, usually in a single line that names Murugan as the nephew of the Lord who brought it about. He speaks of Hari who let fly a single arrow so that Ravana fell with all his clan (song 19, Thiruparankundram), of the one who destroyed Ravana (song 597, Thiruchengode), and of Rama who loosed his arrow (song 707, Kodainagar). In song 959, at Madurai, the same Lord who cut down the demon who had wronged Janaki is the one who returned in victory to Ayodhya.
