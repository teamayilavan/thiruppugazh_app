The two tests at Madurai ended with the leaf of Sambandar's hymn recovered from the Vaigai at Thiruvedagam. The Periya Puranam then tells what followed. The king, seeing the Jains defeated in the contest they themselves had demanded, turned to his minister and declared that, since they had lost and had earlier done grave wrong against the saint, they should be put to death on the stake (Periya Puranam, Sambandar Puranam 853). Sambandar, the Puranam says, though he bore them no enmity, did not prevent the king's order, because of the wrong done to the monastery where the devotees of Shiva had slept (v. 854). The minister carried out the sentence, and Sekkizhar states that the eight thousand Jains of the eight hills went to the stakes (v. 855). The next verse treats the stakes as pillars of victory for the saint (v. 856).

This hard tradition is not softened in the Puranam, and it is repeated in Shaiva tradition and in the Thiruppugazh. Many readers today find it painful. It is recorded here because the texts record it; the film made from this story does not show it.

What the Puranam presents as the true end of the contest follows at once. Sambandar gave the holy ash to the king, who bowed, received it and put it on his whole body; and when the people of Madurai heard that the king had put on the ash, they all did the same (v. 857). With the king made pure by the ash, the Puranam says, the Vedic way prevailed again throughout the land, and the darkness of the Jain teaching passed away (v. 858). The queen Mangaiyarkkarasi and the minister Kulachchirai, who had first called the saint to Madurai, are honoured by Sekkizhar throughout the episode for keeping their devotion in a hostile court.

Arunagiri mentions the end of the Jains at Madurai in some forty songs: twenty are collected with the Shiva set, and twenty-one are gathered here, a few of them in both. Usually it is a single line in a list of Murugan's deeds as Sambandar: the one who sent the Jains to the stakes and spread the holy ash in Madurai (song 1162 is typical). In these songs the event is part of a larger image, the restoration of the holy ash and of Tamil Shaivism through the child saint, rather than a story told for its own sake.
