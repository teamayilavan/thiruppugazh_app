The wooing of Valli is told at length in the Daksha Kandam of the Kanda Puranam of Kachiyappa Sivachariyar, in the padalam named for Valli's marriage (verses 1801 to 2067), and it is the source of the tale that the Thiruppugazh alludes to in some twenty-five songs.

Valli is the daughter of Nambi, chief of the hunter people of the hills, and she keeps watch over the millet field from a platform among the crop, driving off the parrots and other birds (verse 1851). Murugan comes down from his hill to Tiruttani (1854) and from there to her mountain (1866). He first appears to her as a tall young hunter with a striped bow and arrows, an anklet at his foot and a garland on his shoulders (1865), and he speaks to her of his longing (1872 to 1874). When her father comes with the hunters, Murugan stands in the field as a Vengai tree. The hunters, astonished to find a tree where none had stood before, fear that it brings some harm, and some would cut it down; but Nambi sees nothing wrong, and leaves with his men, and Murugan takes human form again (1875 to 1882).

As the hunters return he takes the guise of an aged ascetic of the Saiva path (1894), greets Nambi and gives him holy ash (1895), and then asks Valli for food and for water, which she gives him (1900 to 1904). When the old man declares his love for her she is outraged and tells him to be gone (1905 to 1909). Murugan then thinks of his elder brother, the elephant-faced Vinayaka, who comes roaring as a wild elephant (1910 to 1911). Frightened, Valli runs back to the old man and clings to him, begging him to save her from the elephant (1912), and Murugan thanks his brother, who withdraws (1914 to 1915). Murugan then shows his true form, with twelve arms and six faces, the spear and the peacock (1916), and tells her that in an earlier birth, as the daughter of the Lord who swallowed the world, she had done penance to win him (1919).

The wedding takes place in her father's house in the hunters' village, with Nambi giving her hand to Murugan and the sage Narada arranging the rites (1996 to 2000). The text names the Vengai tree; some English commentaries on the songs speak of a neem tree, but no neem appears in this padalam.
