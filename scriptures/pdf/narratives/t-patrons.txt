In several songs Arunagiri looks back with regret on verses of praise addressed to rich men in the hope of a gift. To flatter such a patron, a poet would compare him to the great givers of the old Tamil country, above all to Pari and Kari.

Song 306, one of the songs for Kundruthoradal, the shrines on every hill, describes people who chase the money of foolish and deceitful misers and compare them to the famous givers Pari and Kari as if they were to be worshipped; the poet asks to be spared this and to sing of Murugan's feet. In song 436, sung at Thiruvannamalai, he confesses that he once told people that they were like the wish-giving tree, like the rain cloud, like King Pari, and like Karna, the giver of the Mahabharata. Song 935 mocks the flattery of calling a patron equal to the twenty-one celebrated givers. In songs 169 and 175 a patron is likewise hailed as a second Pari, a cloud that showers gifts.

Pari, Kari and the others are real figures of the Sangam age, and the poems that praise them are preserved in the Purananuru. Pari, the chieftain of the hill of Parambu, is the subject of a group of poems by his friend Kapilar (Purananuru 105 to 111). In one of them Kapilar tells the three crowned kings besieging the hill that it will not be taken by force: Pari's land had three hundred villages, and all three hundred have been given to those who came asking; only Pari and the poet remain, and the hill (Purananuru 110). Kari is praised in poems 121 to 124 and 126, Ay in 127 to 130, Nalli in 148 to 150, Kumanan in 151 and Ori in 204. Of Pekan the poet Paranar says that he gave his cloak to a peacock, knowing that a peacock would neither wear it nor wrap itself in it, because his giving looked at another's need and not at reward (Purananuru 141); and that he gave like the rain, which falls even on salt land (142).

The famous tale that Pari gave his chariot to a jasmine creeper that had nothing to climb on belongs to the tradition around these poems, and is not in the Purananuru poems cited here. Karna and the twenty-one givers named in the songs are not Sangam patrons.

For Arunagiri the great givers are the measure against which the misers of his day, and his own flattery, are found wanting. The true giver, the songs say, is Murugan, and the poet's verse belongs to him (songs 169, 175, 306, 436, 935).
