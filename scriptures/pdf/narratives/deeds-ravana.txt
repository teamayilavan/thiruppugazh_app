The story of Ravana and Kailasa is told in the last book of the Ramayana, where the sage Agastya recounts Ravana's early life to Rama (Ramayana 7.16). Ravana, returning from his conquests in the flying chariot Pushpaka, found that the chariot would not pass over a certain mountain. Nandi, Shiva's attendant, told him to turn back, because Shiva was there. Ravana scorned Nandi and mocked his monkey-like face, and Nandi warned that beings with faces like his would one day destroy Ravana. Angered, Ravana set his arms beneath the mountain and lifted it, so that it shook. Shiva, without effort, pressed the mountain down with his toe, crushing Ravana's arms beneath it, and Ravana let out a cry that shook the three worlds. On his ministers' advice he praised Shiva with hymns, and Shiva, pleased, released him and gave him the name Ravana, "the one who roared" (Ramayana 7.16).

The Tamil saints loved this story more than almost any other, and often used it to show that even the proudest can be forgiven. Sambandar sings that Ravana, pressed down, cried that Shiva's feet were his only refuge and sang to him with his own sinews as strings (Tevaram 3.28.8). Appar says that a single finger pressed him down, that he cried out and sang with a nerve as a string, and was released (Tevaram 4.49.10); that Ravana, sure of his boon of not dying, was crushed by the toe and then moved the Lord with his music (Tevaram 6.20.10); and that the Lord, having crushed the proud king, showed him mercy (Tevaram 5.72.10, 4.1.10). Sambandar too says that after breaking Ravana's strength with one toe, the Lord gave him grace (Tevaram 3.5.8, 2.20.9, 2.59.8). So familiar was the story that Sambandar usually gives it the eighth verse of a hymn, and Appar often the tenth.

The pattern the saints see in it is clear: pride lifted against God is pressed down without effort, and a single sincere song is enough to turn punishment into grace.

Arunagiri touches the story in a few songs, where Kailasa is named as the mountain that Ravana lifted from its roots. He recalls it in song 312 (Kanchipuram), in song 485 (Chidambaram), and in song 522, sung for Kailasa itself, where he speaks of the mountain that was proudly uprooted and lifted. In each case the story honours the abode of Murugan's father.
