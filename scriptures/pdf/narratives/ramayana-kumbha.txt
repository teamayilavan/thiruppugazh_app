After Kumbhakarna had fallen, his sons went out to battle to avenge him, and the eldest, Kumbha, proved a formidable archer. In Valmiki's account Kumbha wounds and drives back several of the leading monkey warriors, among them Angada, until Sugriva, the monkey king, comes against him in person. Sugriva breaks Kumbha's bow, and the two fight with their bare hands, until Sugriva strikes Kumbha a mighty blow on the chest and fells him (Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha 76.92–93).

Kamban tells Kumbha's death inside the section on Atikaya, another son of Ravana's house who fell in the same phase of the war. He describes the king of the monkeys and the victorious Kumbha wheeling around each other a thousand times on the battlefield, fighting with trees and with maces, clashing like mountains (Kamba Ramayanam, Atikayan Vadhai Padalam 236). When Sugriva, son of the sun, at last closes with him, his strong hand falls as a smith's hammer beats iron in the forge, and the chest of the rakshasa splits like a mountain (Atikayan Vadhai Padalam 245). Kamban follows this with a fiercer image of the end (246), which is better left out of a retelling for a general audience.

Unlike most of the great deaths of the war, this one is not Rama's deed. It belongs to Sugriva, the friend whom Rama had restored to his throne, and it shows the monkey king repaying that friendship with his own courage. No Divya Prabandham verse on Kumbha's death was found for this study.

Arunagiri mentions Kumbha only once, in song 835 (Enkan), and there the deed is not Sugriva's. The song praises the cloud-dark Lord who stood in fierce wrath and won with his arrows, so that "Kumban" too was shaken, after Lanka had been burnt by "that monkey", Hanuman. Arunagiri's "Kumban" may mean Kumbha or may stand for his father Kumbhakarna; the song does not make it clear. As always, the purpose of the line is to praise Vishnu as Rama, whose nephew Murugan is, and the whole war is seen as that Lord's victory.
