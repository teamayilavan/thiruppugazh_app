On their way to Mithila, the sage Vishwamitra brought the young princes Rama and Lakshmana to an empty hermitage that had once belonged to the sage Gautama. There, he told them, Gautama's wife Ahalya had long been held under a curse, after Indra, the king of the gods, came to her in the form of her husband and deceived her. The two great poets of the Ramayana tell what followed in different ways.

In Valmiki's Ramayana, Ahalya is not a stone. Under Gautama's curse she has lived for long years in the hermitage itself, unseen by all beings, in penance, waiting for Rama's coming. When Rama enters, she becomes visible again, shining, and the two princes touch her feet in reverence; she receives them as honoured guests, and Gautama returns to her (Valmiki Ramayana, Bala 49.16–17). In this telling the honour is shown by the princes to her.

Kamban's Tamil Ramayana gives the picture that the South knows best. Ahalya has become a stone, and when the dust of Rama's feet falls on it, the stone loses its long dullness and she stands again in her former form (Kamba Ramayanam, Akalikai Padalam 14). Vishwamitra then tells Rama her story, speaking of Indra's wrongdoing and of the punishment Gautama laid on him (Akalikai Padalam 15). The image of a woman restored by the dust of the Lord's feet became one of the best loved in Tamil devotion. In the Divya Prabandham commentary on Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 4.1.3 it is even said, with gentle humour, that Rama kept to the shore lest the stones under his feet turn into women as Ahalya had.

Both tellings treat Ahalya with dignity. Her story is one of release rather than blame: a long, patient waiting that ends the moment the Lord passes by.

Arunagiri uses the story in two songs, each time in the line that names Murugan as the nephew of Vishnu. In song 483 (Chidambaram) he speaks of the golden foot that touched the stone so that it took on a fair form, and calls Rama the Raghava who came to end her disgrace. In song 669 (Virinchipuram) he praises the lotus foot of the Lord by which Ahalya, who had the form of a stone, became a woman again. Both follow Kamban's image of the stone and the touch of the feet.
