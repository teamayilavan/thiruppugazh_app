The Bharata war, fought over eighteen days on the plain of Kurukshetra between the Pandavas and their cousins the Kauravas, is the centre of the Mahabharata. Its course fills five books of the epic, from the Bhishma Parva to the Sauptika Parva, and no single verse can stand for it. Krishna refuses to take up arms but serves as Arjuna's charioteer, and at the outset, between the two armies, speaks to him the Bhagavad Gita, which forms part of the Bhishma Parva. Through the war the great warriors fall one by one: Bhishma, who lies on a bed of arrows; Drona and Karna; and finally Duryodhana, struck down by Bhima's mace. The single episodes in which Krishna's part is most decisive are told in the neighbouring stories of this book.

The Bhagavata Purana, looking back at the war from the opening of its eleventh book, gives it a theological frame. It says that Krishna, having killed many demons, arranged the great battle between the Kurus and the Pandavas "to remove the burden of the earth", and that he used the Pandavas, enraged by the cheating at dice, the insults and the seizing of Draupadi's hair, as the immediate cause (Bhagavata Purana 11.1.1–2). The kings with their armies who had become a burden to the earth were destroyed through the war (11.1.3).

Arunagiri refers to the war in at least eleven songs, almost always in this spirit, and almost always through Krishna's role as charioteer. In song 339, sung at Kanchipuram, the whole land of the Kurus and the warrior clans perish in eighteen days while Achyuta drives the chariot. Song 1060 says that the hundred sons of Dhritarashtra fell in "three times six" days, while the Lord who drove the chariot protected Partha (Arjuna). Song 1196 praises the Mayan who drove the chariot yoked with the horses of victorious Vijaya, and song 930, sung at Neruvur, wonders at the humility of the Lord who drove Vijaya's chariot as the hundred kings were broken. Song 1056 echoes the Bhagavata closely, saying that when the horses, elephants and chariots of Dhritarashtra's sons were destroyed, the Lord "removed the sorrow of the earth". Song 541, sung at Thirukkazhukundram, gives Krishna, the bearer of the discus, a darker role, as the one who stirred the enmity that would destroy sons and kings alike. In all of them the war is not celebrated for its victories; it is remembered as the work of the Lord whose nephew Murugan is.
