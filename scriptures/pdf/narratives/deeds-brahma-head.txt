Two Puranic traditions explain why Brahma, the creator, has four heads rather than five. In the Shiva Purana the story follows the episode of the pillar of fire: Brahma falsely claims to have seen the top of the pillar, and Bhairava, brought forth from Shiva's brow, seizes Brahma's fifth head as the punishment for his lie (Shiva Purana, Vidyesvara-samhita, chapter 8). In the Skanda Purana it is pride rather than a lie: Brahma's fifth head blazes with arrogance, and Kalabhairava plucks it off with a fingernail (Skanda Purana, Kashi-khanda, chapter 31).

The deed has a consequence. Brahma is a brahmin, and the Shiva Purana tells that after cutting off the head Bhairava wandered through the three worlds with the skull in his hand, keeping the Kapalika vow, pursued by Brahmahatya, the personified guilt of killing a brahmin (Shiva Purana, Satarudra-samhita, chapter 9). The skull becomes his begging bowl, which links this story to Shiva's form as Bhikshatana, the divine beggar. Where the wandering ended is told differently in different places. The Kashi-khanda, a work in praise of Varanasi, brings Bhairava to that city; Tamil tradition associates the deed with the temple of Kandiyur, which Appar names as the place of the creator's head (Tevaram 4.93.3).

The Tamil hymns mention the deed often and briefly. Manikkavasagar says that the head of Brahma on the lotus was cut off with a nail (Tiruvasagam, Tiruvundiyar, 14.18). Sambandar calls the Lord the one who cut off Brahma's head and who holds it as a skull (Tevaram 3.89.5, 2.19.3). Appar says he took one of Brahma's five heads with his hand (Tevaram 6.66.8, 6.50.10) and begged his food with Brahma's skull (Tevaram 6.34.6). Sundarar places it beside the crushing of Ravana and the drinking of the poison as one of the Lord's great acts (Tevaram 7.4.8). Tirumular counts it among the eight heroic deeds commemorated at the eight Virattam temples (Tirumandiram, second tantra, section 2, verse 2).

Arunagiri uses the story as a title of Shiva. He calls him the Lord who cut off Brahma's head (song 750, Vriddhachalam) and the one who plucked Brahma's crown with his nail (song 797, Thiruvidaikkazhi), and he names the deed in long lists of his father's victories, beside the burning of Kama and the three cities (songs 188, 577). Brahma's lost head is, for Arunagiri, a sign that pride, even in a god, cannot stand before Shiva, and in the songs Murugan, too, humbles Brahma, imprisoning him when he cannot explain the meaning of Om.
