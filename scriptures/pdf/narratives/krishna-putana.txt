Putana is the first of the demons that Kamsa, king of Mathura, sent against the infant Krishna. The Bhagavata Purana describes her as an ogress engaged by Kamsa to kill babies, who wandered through towns and villages doing her work (Bhagavata Purana 10.6.2). Moving through the sky, she took the form of a beautiful woman and entered Gokula, the village of Nanda (10.6.4–6). No one stopped her. She came into Nanda's house and found the child asleep. Krishna, knowing why she had come, closed his eyes, and Yashoda and Rohini, overwhelmed by her beauty and her motherly manner, let her take him on her lap; the Bhagavata compares her to a sharp sword in a soft sheath (10.6.7–9).

She put her breast, smeared with poison, into the baby's mouth. Krishna pressed it with both hands and drank out her life along with the milk. Crying "Let me go, let me go!", sweating and flailing, she screamed so loudly that the earth and sky shook and people fell down in fear, and then she fell dead in her true and gigantic form (10.6.10–13), crushing the trees for miles around (10.6.14). The women found the child playing on her body without fear and performed rites of protection over him (10.6.18–20). When the villagers burned the body, the smoke was fragrant, because the Lord had drunk from her; the Bhagavata draws the moral that even one who came to kill him, by offering him her breast, reached the highest end (10.6.34–36).

This is one of the stories the Alvars love most. Periyazhwar pictures the infant looking steadily at Putana, knowing she had come to harm him (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 2.10.6), and marvels that the child who drank the poisoned milk of the cruel ogress still obeys his cowherd mother (3.5.9). In Andal's dawn song the sleepers are woken with the name of the one who drank the demoness's poisoned milk (Tiruppavai 6).

Arunagiri refers to the story in two songs. Song 1001 praises the incomparable Lord who drank the life of the ogress together with her milk, and whose feet grew to measure the seven worlds. Song 626, sung at Kundrakudi, recalls the messengers that Kamsa sent against the child, and names Murugan the nephew of the one who won the fight against the ogress. The baby who is never in danger becomes, in these lines, a sign of the Lord's power hidden in the smallest form.
