Kamsa, king of Mathura, had been warned that the eighth child of his sister Devaki would kill him, and he spent the years of Krishna's childhood sending demons to Gokula to destroy the boy. At last he invited Krishna and Balarama to a festival at Mathura, intending that his wrestlers should kill them in the arena. The tenth book of the Bhagavata Purana tells how the brothers killed his elephant Kuvalayapida at the gate and entered the arena carrying its tusks (Bhagavata Purana 10.43.15–16).

Krishna wrestled the giant Chanura and Balarama wrestled Mushtika, while the women of the city protested that it was unjust to set such huge men against two tender boys (10.44.7–10). Both wrestlers fell, the others fled, and the brothers danced with their cowherd friends (10.44.22–29). Kamsa alone did not rejoice. He silenced the music and ordered the boys driven out, the cowherds' property seized, Nanda arrested, and Vasudeva and his own father Ugrasena killed (10.44.31–33).

As he raved, Krishna sprang onto the royal dais. Kamsa took up a sword and shield and moved from side to side "like a hawk in the sky", but Krishna seized him as Garuda seizes a snake, caught him by the hair, knocked off his crown and threw him down from the dais onto the wrestling ground (10.44.34–37). The Bhagavata adds a striking reflection: because Kamsa, in fear, had seen the Lord before him while eating, moving, sleeping and breathing, he attained a form like the Lord's (10.44.39). Kamsa's brothers attacked and were struck down by Balarama; the queens came weeping, and Krishna consoled them and arranged the funeral rites, and then freed his parents and bowed at their feet (10.44.40–50). The Alvars remember the same scene: Periyazhwar tells of Kamsa's wives wailing after his death (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 4.5.8), and Andal says that the plot Kamsa laid against the child turned back on Kamsa himself (Tiruppavai 25).

Arunagiri recalls the story in a few songs, always briefly. In song 1274 he names Kamsa as the wicked uncle who plotted murder and did not treat the child as his nephew. Song 851 speaks of Hari, who took Kamsa's life as he had broken the twin maruda trees, and song 615 calls Murugan the nephew of the cloud-dark Lord who brought down the warlike Kamsa. In each case the story serves, as so often in the Thiruppugazh, to praise Murugan as the beloved nephew of that Lord.
