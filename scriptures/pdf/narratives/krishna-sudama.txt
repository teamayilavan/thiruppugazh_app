The story of Krishna's poor brahmin friend is told in the eightieth and eighty-first chapters of the tenth book of the Bhagavata Purana. The text calls him simply a brahmin friend of Krishna, learned in the Vedas, detached from pleasures, peaceful and self-controlled (Bhagavata Purana 10.80.6). He lived as a householder on whatever came to him unasked, and his wife, sharing his poverty, was thin with hunger (10.80.7). The Bhagavata describes him as poorly clad, and the southern name Kuchela, "the man in the worn cloth", comes from this; the name Sudama by which he is widely known belongs to later tradition.

His wife, trembling, reminded him that the husband of the goddess of fortune was his friend and urged him to go to Dwaraka (10.80.8–11). He agreed, thinking that simply to see Krishna would be the greatest gain, and asked if there was anything in the house to take as a gift. She begged four handfuls of flat, beaten rice from the neighbours and tied them in a torn cloth (10.80.12–14). He set out, wondering all the way how he would ever be admitted, and passed three guard posts and three gates to reach the palace (10.80.15–17).

Krishna was seated on his queen's couch. Seeing his friend in the distance, he rose at once, went forward and embraced him, shedding tears of love (10.80.18–19). He seated him on the couch, washed his feet and sprinkled the water on his own head, while the goddess of fortune herself fanned the thin brahmin in his torn clothes, and the people of the palace wondered what this unkempt man had done to deserve such honour (10.80.20–26). The two friends took each other's hands and spoke of their days in the school of their teacher Sandipani, and Krishna recalled the night they were sent for firewood and were caught in a storm in the forest, until their teacher found them in the morning and blessed them (10.80.27–42).

The next chapter tells how Krishna took the bundle of rice that his friend was too ashamed to offer and ate from it with delight, and how the brahmin went home without asking for anything, only to find his hut replaced by a palace. No Alvar verse on this story was found in the course of this research.

Arunagiri's single reference is in song 355, sung at Thiruvanaikka, where he calls Murugan the nephew of Gopala, the Lord of a thousand names who plays the flute, whose love for his friend never changed. The reading of the line as a reference to this friend is the most likely one, though the wording is brief. It is the friendship itself, not the riches, that the song remembers.
