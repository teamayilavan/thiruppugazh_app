When the gods and the demons churned the ocean of milk for the nectar of immortality, using a mountain as the churning rod and the serpent Vasuki as the rope, the first thing to rise from the waters was not nectar but a terrible poison, Halahala. The Bhagavata Purana tells how it spread in every direction and burned the worlds, and how the frightened creators went to Shiva for refuge. Shiva spoke to Bhavani, his consort, of his compassion for living beings; she, knowing his power, consented. He gathered the poison in his palm and drank it, and it left a dark blue mark on his throat, which became an ornament to him (Bhagavata Purana 8.7.19–46).

The Ramayana tells the story in a different light. There it is the venom of Vasuki that rises and burns the world, and Vishnu asks Shiva to accept it as the first thing born of the churning, his rightful share; Shiva drinks it as if it were nectar and returns to Kailasa (Ramayana 1.45.19–26). Elsewhere the Ramayana mentions in passing Shiva's throat, made blue by the poison of the churning (Ramayana 1.36.5). The Skanda Purana also tells of the churning and of Shiva swallowing the poison (Skanda Purana, Maheshvara-khanda, Kedara-khanda, chapters 9–10). The well-known detail that Parvati held his throat so that the poison stopped there is found in popular and temple tradition rather than in these passages.

For the Tamil saints the blue throat is the very sign of Shiva's mercy. Sambandar describes the serpent as rope and the mountain as rod, the poison rising and the gods fleeing, and the Lord who drank it so that the gods could have the nectar (Tevaram 2.91.7, 2.95.6, 2.45.4). Appar says the gods took refuge and he drank it (Tevaram 4.65.2), that he swallowed it in the blink of an eye and his throat grew dark (Tevaram 6.83.8). Sundarar says he drank it to save the gods and held it in his throat (Tevaram 7.55.5). Manikkavasagar puts it most plainly: had he not eaten that poison, Brahma, Vishnu and all the gods would have died (Tiruvasagam, Tiruchchazhal, 12.8).

Arunagiri recalls the poison in some fifteen songs, usually naming Shiva as the Lord who swallowed the poison of the sea, and so Murugan as the son of that merciful father. Examples are songs 27, 46 and 99 (Thiruchendur), 197 (Palani), 264 (Thiruthani) and 907 (Vayalur).
