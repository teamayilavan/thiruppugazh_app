The Bhagavata Purana tells that a pool of the Yamuna near Vrindavan had been poisoned by the serpent Kaliya, so that its water boiled, birds flying over it fell in, and the wind from it killed the plants and creatures on the bank (Bhagavata Purana 10.16.4–5). To purify the river, Krishna climbed a tall kadamba tree on the bank and leapt into the pool (10.16.6). As he played in the water, Kaliya rose in anger, bit him on the chest and wrapped him in his coils; seeing him motionless, his cowherd friends fell to the ground in grief and the cows cried out in fear (10.16.8–11).

Krishna freed himself and, having tired the serpent by circling him, pressed down his raised hoods, climbed onto them and began to dance, while the gods and heavenly singers played and sang and showered flowers (10.16.26–27). Whenever the serpent lifted a head in anger, Krishna danced it down, until Kaliya was broken and recognised him as the Lord, taking shelter in him (10.16.29–30). The Bhagavata speaks of the serpent's many heads, not the five that later art usually shows. Kaliya's wives came with folded hands and pleaded for their husband, saying that his punishment was itself a mercy (10.16.31–34). Krishna spared him and told him to go to the ocean with his family, promising that Garuda would no longer harm him, since he bore the Lord's footprints (10.16.60–63); Kaliya worshipped him with garments, jewels and garlands and left, and the Yamuna's water became pure again (10.16.64–67).

The Alvars sing of the same scene. Periyazhwar tells of the child who stirred the pond so that Kaliya rose spitting poison (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 2.10.3) and who leapt on the serpent's spread hoods in the poisoned river (1.8.3); Thirumangai Alvar of the Lord who danced on Kaliya's head and made it tremble (Periya Thirumozhi 5.1.10); and Andal of the one who jumped from the flowering tree onto Kaliya's head (Nachiyar Thirumozhi 4.4).

For Arunagiri the scene is above all a dance. In song 163 (Palani) Hari dances on the golden crest of the serpent to the beat of "thimitha thimi thimi"; in song 620 (Poomparai) the dance on the serpent's hoods keeps its rhythm of "tha thimi thothi"; in song 881 (Thirukkurangaduthurai) the poison-swollen hoods are struck as he dances; and in song 1099 the jewelled hoods sway beneath his lotus feet. Each time Murugan is praised as the nephew of the dancing Lord.
