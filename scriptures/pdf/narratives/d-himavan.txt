In the Thiruppugazh the Goddess is again and again "the daughter of Himavan", the king of the snowy mountains. The Kanda Puranam of Kachiyappa Sivachariyar tells how she came to be his daughter. Uma had once been born as the daughter of Daksha, who scorned Shiva, and had grown up bearing his name. She came before Shiva and asked to be freed from that body and that name (Utpatti Kandam 375–377). Shiva told her that Himavan, the king of the Himalaya, was performing a hard penance, and that she should go to him as a small child, grow up there and perform her own penance; when she had done so, he would come with the gods and the hosts and marry her (380–383). She bowed and left Kailasa for the Himalaya (384–385).

High on the mountain, beside a pool, Himavan had been performing penance, wishing that Gauri should become his daughter and that he might give her to Shiva (394). The Goddess appeared there as an infant lying on a lotus that opened in the pool (395). Seeing her, the mountain-king wept with joy, sang and danced, and worshipped her, saying that he was saved (396–397). He lifted her, carried her on his head to his palace and gave her into the hands of his wife Menai, who heard all that had happened and rejoiced (398–400). They adorned her and raised her lovingly, as the moon grows (401–402).

When five years had passed, the girl asked her father to let her go to a separate place on the mountain with companions and perform penance to win Shiva (404–405). Himavan said that she was far too young (406). She laughed and answered that Shiva alone protects, and no one else can; so he agreed, built her a hermitage on the mountain and gave her companions, and she began her penance (407–410). Elsewhere in the same kandam Shiva, coming in disguise as an old man, speaks of the daughter of the mountain-king who is performing penance (640).

Arunagiri uses this identity of the Goddess in a great many songs, most often just before he says that she gave birth to Murugan or nursed him. He calls her Himavan's daughter, the peahen of the mountain and Imayavalli, the creeper of the Himalaya (songs 32, 107, 239, 291). Song 354 says, as the Puranam does, that she was given to Himavan as a boon for his long penance, and songs 60, 63 and 137 name her as the mountain-king's daughter who nursed Murugan.
