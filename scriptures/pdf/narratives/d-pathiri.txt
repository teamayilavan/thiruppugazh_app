Thiruppathirippuliyur, now part of the town of Cuddalore on the Coromandel coast, takes its name from the pathiri, a tree with pale, fragrant, trumpet-shaped flowers. According to the temple's own tradition, the Goddess came here to perform penance beneath a pathiri tree, wishing to be one with Shiva and never separated from him. The tradition adds that she had already worshipped him at a thousand and seven other places before she came here, that she worshipped in a formless state, and that the shrine marks the spot with a bare pedestal on which there is no image. These details come from the temple's sthala purana as it is told today, and they should be read as temple legend.

The shrine is old, and was praised by the Tevaram poets. Appar's hymn to Thiruppathirippuliyur (Tevaram 4.94) speaks of the Lord who dwells here so that his love for the daughter of the mountain may abide, the Lord of Thiruppathirippuliyur whom all the land bows to (4.94, verse 4), and of the one who drank the poison of the ocean at the gods' request and burned the three cities of the asuras (verse 7). The hymn names the place and the Goddess, but it does not tell the story of her penance.

The Thiruppugazh does. In song 745, sung at this shrine, Arunagiri says that Parvati, the purest form of the Mother, once performed a unique penance in the great town of Padalanagar, which is Thiruppathirippuliyur, where Murugan dwells with joy. In song 1162, one of the general songs, he gathers a long garland of the Goddess's names: she is the one adorned with knowledge, the colour of the dark cloud, the cause of all things, the enchantress who stirs desire in all lives, the one with the complexion of emerald, and the Goddess who did penance in the shade of a pathiri tree. She is Parvati, possessed of every virtue, and Uma whose consort is Shiva.

The penance under the pathiri tree is thus known to us from the songs and from the temple tradition, while the older hymn of the shrine testifies to the worship of Shiva and the mountain's daughter in this place. For Arunagiri it is one more sign of the Goddess's devotion to Shiva, and of her nearness to Murugan, who dwells in the same town. The songs to cite are 745 (Thiruppathirippuliyur) and 1162 (general songs).
