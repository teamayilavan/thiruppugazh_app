In the forest of Kishkindha, Rama made an alliance with the exiled monkey prince Sugriva: Rama would help him recover his kingdom and his wife from his elder brother Vali, and Sugriva would help Rama find Sita. When Sugriva first went to Kishkindha and challenged Vali, Rama stood hidden with his bow, but the two brothers, fighting, looked so alike that he could not tell them apart, and he held back his arrow for fear of killing his friend. Sugriva, badly beaten, fled back to the forest and reproached Rama for not keeping his word.

Rama explained the reason, and then supplied a simple remedy. Valmiki tells how he had a flowering gaja creeper pulled down and hung around Sugriva's neck, so that he could be recognised in the fight, and how, wearing it, Sugriva shone in the evening light (Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha 12.39–41). Kamban keeps the same detail: Rama tells Sugriva to go wearing a flowering creeper, and Sugriva goes back and challenges his brother again (Kamba Ramayanam, Vali Vadhai Padalam 61).

The episode is small, but it carries weight. It shows Rama's care not to harm his friend, and it prepares the second fight, in which Vali is killed by Rama's arrow. Because that killing, made from hiding, has been questioned and defended through the centuries, the garland is often remembered as the sign that marked out the friend Rama had promised to protect.

The Alvars, who tell the killing of Vali several times, do not single out the garland itself in the verses read for this study, and no Divya Prabandham verse is cited here for it.

Arunagiri recalls the garland once, in song 266 (Thiruthani). There he praises the Lord who put a garland of kanthal flowers on one monkey and sent him to the front, and with it brought down the other monkey king. In the song the creeper of the epics becomes kanthal, the glory lily, a flower beloved in Tamil poetry and especially associated with Murugan's hills. The song calls that Lord, Achyuta, the one of perfect virtue, and names Murugan as his nephew.
