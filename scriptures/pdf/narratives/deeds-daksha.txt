The Bhagavata Purana tells the story in a sequence of chapters. At an assembly of the creators, all rose to greet Daksha except Brahma and Shiva; Daksha took offence and cursed his son-in-law, calling him unworthy of a share in the sacrifice (Bhagavata Purana 4.2.1–19). Later Daksha held a great sacrifice of his own and did not invite Shiva. His daughter Sati, Shiva's wife, went to it against her husband's advice; finding no welcome and hearing her husband slighted, she gave up her body in yogic fire (4.2–4.5). Shiva in his grief and anger brought forth Virabhadra, who with Shiva's hosts wrecked the sacrifice and cut off Daksha's head (4.5.1–20). Brahma then went with the gods to Shiva and appeased him; the sacrifice was completed, and Daksha was restored to life with the head of a goat (4.6–4.7).

The story is old and widely told. The Mahabharata speaks of Rudra destroying Daksha's sacrifice (Mahabharata 12,274.18 ff. and 12,330.42–44), and the Ramayana adds a detail that matters later in the epic: at Daksha's sacrifice Rudra strung his bow in anger at the gods who had denied him a share, and when he was appeased he gave the bow away. It is this bow that comes down to King Janaka and that Rama breaks to win Sita (Ramayana 1.66.9–12). The Linga Purana (1.99–100), the Shiva Purana (Rudra-samhita, Sati-khanda, chapters 26–37) and the Skanda Purana (Maheshvara-khanda, Kedara-khanda, chapters 1–4) all tell the story in full.

The Tamil saints return to it often. Tirumular gives it a section of nine verses, in which Shiva's wrath rises from the fire and the gods scatter (Tirumandiram, second tantra, section 4, verse 1), and he says that without the grace of Hara even Vishnu, Brahma and Daksha lose head and hand (verse 6). Manikkavasagar asks why the Lord cut off the heads of Daksha and of the sacrifice, and answers that it was so he could then give them grace (Tiruvasagam, Tiruchchazhal, 12.5; see also 8.15 and 14.16). Appar recalls the day he destroyed Daksha's great sacrifice (Tevaram 6.34.4, 6.85.7), and Sambandar places it beside the kicking of Yama (Tevaram 1.113.6).

Arunagiri recalls the deed as one of Shiva's titles. He speaks of the Lord who cut off the head of the angry Daksha (song 458, Chidambaram), of Daksha who was ended in former days (song 904, Vayalur), and of the sacrifice of the hostile Daksha (songs 309, Kanchipuram, and 461 and 491, Chidambaram). The lesson he draws is the one the saints drew: an offering that leaves out the Lord is empty.
