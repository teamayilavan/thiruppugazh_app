Ravana's plan to carry off Sita depended on drawing Rama and Lakshmana away from their hermitage at Panchavati. For this he turned to the rakshasa Maricha, a master of illusion, who had once felt the force of Rama's arrows when he attacked Vishwamitra's sacrifice and had been flung far away. Maricha tried to dissuade Ravana, but under threat of death he agreed.

Maricha took the form of a wonderful golden deer and appeared near the hermitage. Kamban describes him becoming the deer in order to reach the one who was herself like a gentle deer (Kamba Ramayanam, Maricha Vadhai Padalam 43), and adds that Sita, guileless, did not see through the trick (47). She longed for the deer and asked Rama to catch it. Lakshmana suspected that it was Maricha in disguise, but Rama, leaving Lakshmana to guard Sita, followed the deer deep into the forest, where it led him on and on.

At last Rama shot it. Valmiki describes how Maricha, struck by the arrow, resumed his own form and, dying, cried out in Rama's voice, calling on Sita and Lakshmana (Valmiki Ramayana, Aranya 44.16–23). Kamban has him fall like a hill as he cries out (Maricha Vadhai Padalam 77), and shows Rama realising at once that the cry was Maricha's imitation and grieving for Sita, whom he thinks of as weeping with rain-filled eyes (80). At the hermitage Sita heard the cry and, in her fear for Rama, pressed Lakshmana to go to him. When he had gone, Ravana came in the guise of an ascetic and carried her away.

The Alvars tell the episode among the events of the forest years. Kulasekhara Alvar includes the shooting of Maricha in the form of a deer (Perumal Thirumozhi 10.5), and Periyazhwar recalls the golden deer entering the hermitage, Rama pursuing and shooting it, and its false cry drawing Lakshmana away (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 3.10.7).

Arunagiri recalls the golden deer in song 113 (Palani). There Murugan is the nephew of Achyuta, the Lord of mighty arms who overcame the magic deer and the rakshasas, and who, with his bow, sent the arrow that pierced Vali's chest. In a single line the song gathers the forest episodes together under the name of the Lord whose arrows never missed.
