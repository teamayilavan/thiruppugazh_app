Kuvalayapida was the great elephant of Kamsa, king of Mathura, and its death is the first act of Krishna's last day as a cowherd boy. Kamsa had invited Krishna and Balarama to a festival at Mathura, intending to have them killed. The Bhagavata Purana tells that when the brothers heard the kettledrums of the wrestling arena and went to it, Krishna found Kuvalayapida blocking the entrance at the urging of its keeper (Bhagavata Purana 10.43.1–2).

Krishna tied up his clothes and his curling hair and told the keeper to move aside or he would send both man and elephant to the realm of Yama. The angry keeper drove the elephant at him. It seized Krishna with its trunk, but he slipped free and hid among its legs, and it hunted him by smell (10.43.3–7). The Bhagavata describes the fight almost as play: Krishna dragged the elephant by its tail as Garuda drags a snake, was swung to and fro like a boy pulling a calf, ran in front of it, and fell down on purpose so that its tusks struck the earth (10.43.8–11). When the keepers drove it on once more, he seized its trunk, threw it down, climbed on it "like a lion", pulled out a tusk, and killed the elephant and its keepers with it (10.43.12–14). He then walked into the arena with the tusk on his shoulder; Krishna and Balarama each carried one of the elephant's tusks as a weapon, and when Kamsa saw that Kuvalayapida was dead he was overcome with anxiety (10.43.15–18).

The Tamil Alvars sing of it often. Periyazhwar speaks of Krishna who broke the bow at Kamsa's festival, snapped the elephant's tusks and killed its keeper (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 4.7.7), and of Kamsa's men burning with fear when they saw him after the elephant was killed (4.8.10). Thirumangai Alvar remembers how he broke the white tusk of the angry elephant (Periya Thirumozhi 9.2.6). Where the Bhagavata speaks of one tusk pulled out, the Tamil verses, like the Thiruppugazh, speak of the tusks broken.

Arunagiri uses the story in two songs. In song 158, sung at Palani, Murugan is the nephew of the tall red-eyed Lord who defeated the angry elephant and broke its two tusks, and who played the flute and guarded the cows. Song 1157 joins the elephant to another childhood deed, praising the Lord who made the fierce elephant cry out and who kicked over the cart with his foot.
