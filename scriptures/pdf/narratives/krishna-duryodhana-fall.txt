At the dice game in Hastinapura, when the Pandavas had lost their kingdom and their freedom, Duryodhana insulted Draupadi by baring his thigh to her before the assembly. Bhima then vowed that he would break that thigh with his mace in battle. The Mahabharata recalls this vow at the very moment of its fulfilment, in the duel at the end of the war: "I will break your thighs with the mace in battle," Bhima had said to Suyodhana (Mahabharata, Shalya Parva 9.57.6).

When the eighteen days of fighting were over and his army destroyed, Duryodhana hid in a lake. The Pandavas found him and Yudhishthira challenged him to come out and fight any one of them; he chose the mace and was matched against Bhima. Balarama, Krishna's elder brother, who had taught both men, arrived from his pilgrimage to watch. The two fought long and evenly. Asked by Arjuna which of them was the better, Krishna answered that Bhima was the stronger but Duryodhana the more skilful, and that Bhima could win only by remembering his vow. Arjuna struck his own thigh as a sign, and Bhima, as Duryodhana leapt, brought down the mace and broke his thighs (9.57.44). The blow was below the waist, against the rules of the mace fight, and Balarama raised his weapon in anger until Krishna restrained him.

The episode is among the most troubling in the epic. The Mahabharata does not hide that the victory was won by breaking a rule, and it gives the fallen king the last word in his own defence. The poets and the tradition remember it as the grim end of the war and the keeping of a vow made in the hall of dice.

Arunagiri refers to it once, in song 1135, a general song. There he speaks of Hari, who brought about the victory in the Bharata war in which Suyodhana fell, and praises Murugan as the nephew of that Hari. As elsewhere in the Thiruppugazh, the epic is recalled in a single phrase, as one more deed of Vishnu, and the scene itself is not described.
