In three songs of the Thiruppugazh, Arunagiri describes Ganesha as the one who wrote down the Mahabharata. Song 88, sung at Thiruchendur, speaks of the elephant-faced primeval god who wrote out the long story of the Bharata on the great Mount Meru. Song 1016, one of the general songs, is fuller: knowing the story of the war that Duryodhana was born to wage, Ganesha wrote it on the top of Meru with his single tusk, as the sage Vyasa narrated the epic. Song 1315, sung at Pazhamudircholai, says that he wrote the Mahabharata on the slopes of Meru, breaking his own tusk to write with. In each song the scribe is named only to praise Murugan as his younger brother.

The story is not in the Critical Edition of the Mahabharata. Its first book, the Adi Parva, has an invocation to Ganapati but no passage in which Ganesha writes down the epic. The episode belongs to later versions of the text and to tradition, where it is widely known.

As tradition tells it, Vyasa had composed the whole epic in his mind and needed someone to write it. Ganesha agreed to be his scribe on condition that Vyasa should not pause in his telling; Vyasa in turn asked that Ganesha should understand every verse before he wrote it down. From time to time the sage spoke a verse so dense in meaning that Ganesha had to stop and think, and in that time Vyasa composed what followed. When the pen failed, Ganesha broke off one of his tusks and wrote on with it; this is one of the explanations given for his single tusk. The two conditions are not in the songs, and the account of the tusk varies in the tradition.

What the songs keep is the picture itself: the scribe on Meru, writing with his tusk at the sage's word, so devoted to his task that he gave a part of himself to finish it. Arunagiri uses it, as he uses so many stories of Shiva's household, to name Murugan through his family. Songs 88, 1016 and 1315 each praise Ganesha, the writer of the Bharata, and then turn to Murugan as "the younger brother of that Lord". The broken tusk is never mocked in the songs; it is a mark of the elder brother's devotion to learning.
