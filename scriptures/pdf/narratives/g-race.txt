The race for the fruit is one of the best loved stories of the Tamil country, and at Palani it is told as the reason why the young Murugan stands alone on the hill there. In the Thiruppugazh it appears in only two songs, and in very few words.

Song 30, sung at Thiruchendur, addresses Murugan as the hero on the peacock of red gold who, in the presence of his father, went round the whole world in a contest with the elephant-faced Ganapati. Song 532, sung at Vallimalai, gives the outcome: Murugan flew round the world, and yet the prized fruit was given by Shiva to Ganapati. The same lines go straight on to praise Murugan for teaching the true meaning of the Pranava, the sacred syllable Om, so that even in a tale of losing the poet turns at once to the younger brother's wisdom.

The rest of the tale, as it is commonly told today, belongs to tradition. Ganesha, instead of travelling, walked round his parents, Shiva and Parvati, saying that they were the whole world to him, and so finished first. Murugan, returning, found the prize already given, and went away to the hill of Palani. In the form most often heard, the fruit was brought to Kailasa by the sage Narada, and a popular explanation of the name Palani (in Tamil, Pazhani) takes it from the words "pazham nee", "you yourself are the fruit", said to console the young god. None of these details is in the songs.

Nor is the contest in the Kanda Puranam of Kachiyappa Sivachariyar. Its Utpatti, Daksha and Deva kandams, which tell Murugan's birth, his war and his marriages, were read for any race round the world or any prize fruit, and no such passage was found. The story belongs to later Saiva tradition and to the lore of the Palani shrine, and the two songs of Arunagiri are among its early witnesses.

Arunagiri uses the story lightly, as one of the many names by which Murugan can be called. He does not dwell on the hurt, nor does he say that Murugan left in anger. What he remembers is the swift flight of the peacock round the world (song 30), and, beside the lost fruit, the teaching of Om (song 532): the younger son may have lost the race, but he was the one who could teach the meaning of the sacred sound.
