The story of the crow is told by Sita herself. When Hanuman found her in the Ashoka grove in Lanka and was about to return to Rama, she gave him a private memory as a token, something only she and Rama knew, so that Rama would believe the message. Valmiki places it in her conversation with Hanuman (Valmiki Ramayana, Sundara 38.15–36). Once, near the hill of Chitrakuta, as Rama rested with her, a crow attacked her and tore at her with its claws. The crow was the son of Indra. Rama, seeing that she had been hurt, took a blade of darbha grass, charged it with the power of the weapon of Brahma and sent it after the bird. The crow fled through the worlds, but neither the gods nor its own father could shelter it, and at last it returned and fell at Rama's feet. Rama spared its life, but because the weapon could not be made fruitless, the crow gave up its right eye. Sita then asks why Rama, who used such a weapon on a crow for her sake, has not yet come to rescue her.

Kamban does not tell the episode in his account of Chitrakuta; he brings it in through Sita's memory in the Sundara Kandam. In one verse Sita recalls the day the single arrow was sent against Indra's son and destroyed its eye, so that crows ever after have had only one eye (Kamba Ramayanam, Kaatchi Padalam 28). In another she tells Hanuman how, on the good hill, a crow came and clawed her, and Rama, angered, sent a fierce weapon with a blade of grass from beside a rock, and she asks him to tell it gently (Choodamani Padalam 77).

Periyazhwar also sings of it: at Chitrakuta the crow pecked Sita, and Rama's blade of grass, made a weapon of Brahma, took one of its eyes (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 3.10.6). The tradition there gives the crow the name Jayanta, which is not stated in the verses cited above.

The story is remembered less for the punishment than for the mercy. The offender, who had nowhere else to go, came back to the one he had wronged and was given his life.

Arunagiri recalls it once, in song 228 (Swamimalai). There Murugan is the nephew of Hari, the Mayan, who graciously let the crow keep its life with a single eye. In Arunagiri's line the stress falls on grace: the lost eye is the cost, but the life granted is the gift.
