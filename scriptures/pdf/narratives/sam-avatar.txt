Thirugnana Sambandar, the child saint of Sirkazhi, is one of the three great singers of the Tevaram, and his hymns fill its first three books (Tirumurai 1–3). His life is told at greater length than any other in the Periya Puranam: the Sambandar Puranam runs to 1,256 verses (Periya Puranam, Tirumurai 12). It tells of his birth at Sirkazhi to Sivapadahridayar and Bhagavathiyar (Sambandar Puranam 1–26); of the milk of Shiva-wisdom that Uma fed him from a golden bowl when he was three, and of the first hymn, "Todudaiya Seviyan", that he sang at once (vv. 59–76); of the minstrel Thiruneelakanta Yazhpanar and his wife, who set his hymns to the yazh (vv. 131–141, 444–452); of the pearl palanquin given to him by the Lord of Thiruvarathurai (vv. 195–212); of the cure of the Pandya king's fever with holy ash at Madurai (vv. 599–772); of the palm leaf of his hymn that stayed green in fire and went against the flood of the Vaigai (vv. 773–852); and of Poompavai, raised to life from her bones at Mylapore (vv. 1033–1120). Each of these episodes is told separately in this collection.

The Periya Puranam presents Sambandar as a child born by the grace of Shiva, whose wisdom came directly from the Goddess's milk. It does not say that he was Murugan. That identification belongs to later devotion, and above all to the Thiruppugazh. Arunagiri states it in nearly a hundred songs, usually in one line among the titles of Murugan: the one who came to Pugali, who drank the milk of Uma, who cured the Pandya, who made the leaf go against the river, who gave the world the Tamil Veda. In ninety-five of the 105 songs collected here he says outright that Murugan came as Sambandar, or is Sambandar; a few name the saint without this identification, as the author of the Tevaram or the companion of Yazhpanar. Song 837 gives the idea its fullest form: Murugan hid his twelve shoulders, his peacock and his spear so that no one could recognise him, and was born as a Brahmin child of the Kavuniya family.

For Arunagiri the claim is a devotional one, and it shapes how he understands his own work. The Tevaram, which he calls the Tamil Veda, was in his view sung by Murugan himself; songs 134 (Palani), 261 (Thiruthani), 764 and 774 (Sirkazhi), 913 (Vayalur), 962 (Madurai) and 1317 (Pazhamudircholai) praise Murugan in these terms, and Arunagiri's own songs to Murugan follow, as it were, in the steps of the child of Sirkazhi.
