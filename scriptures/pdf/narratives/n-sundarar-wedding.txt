The story of how Sundarar was claimed by the Lord stands at the opening of his life in the Periya Puranam, in the Thaduthatkonda Puranam ("the story of the one who was stopped and taken"). Nambi Arurar of Thirunavalur, as Sundarar was then known, was about to be married when an old brahmin came into the wedding hall and declared that the bridegroom was his servant (Periya Puranam, Thirumalai Sargam 180–184). Arurar laughed at him and called him a madman (186–187), and demanded to see the deed. When the old man would not hand it over to him, Arurar chased him, seized the palm leaf and tore it up (189–191).

The old man was not disturbed. He said that his home was at Thiruvennainallur and asked that the matter be judged by the brahmins there (193–195). Arurar followed him, drawn, the Puranam says, like iron to a magnet (196). Before the assembly the old man produced the original deed, saying that Arurar's grandfather had written it; the elders read it, compared the writing with other documents, and found it true, and they judged that Arurar was bound to serve him (197–209). When they asked to see his house, the old man led them to the temple of the Lord at Thiruvennainallur, went inside and disappeared (210–212).

Then the Lord appeared and spoke to Arurar: before his birth he had been the Lord's servant, and had been born on earth at the Lord's command; now he was taken back (213). Because he had spoken boldly and harshly, he was to be known as Vanthondan, "the bold servant" (216). The Lord asked him to sing, and since Arurar had called him a madman, he was to begin his song with that word (217–219). So Sundarar sang his first hymn, beginning "Pitha pirai soodi", "O madman, wearing the crescent moon" (220). The "slavery" of the bond is the devotee's servitude to the Lord, and the Tamil Shaiva tradition treasures it for that reason.

Arunagiri tells the story in song 717, sung at Uthiramerur. As the commentary reads it, the Lord took in his hand a deed bearing a royal seal, walked tottering to the wedding, and was rebuked in public by Sundarar, who called him a lying madman; yet, though Sundarar showed him no reverence, the Lord cleverly argued his case and established his claim. Arunagiri asks the same Lord's son, Murugan, to take hold of him too, though he is only an ordinary wanderer.
