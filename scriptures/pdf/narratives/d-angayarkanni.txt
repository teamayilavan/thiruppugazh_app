Madurai, the Tiruvilaiyadal Puranam says, began as a forest of kadamba trees. A merchant named Dhananjayan, returning from trade, was caught there by nightfall and found a lingam shining beneath a heavenly tower; through the night he watched the gods come to worship it in each of the four watches and brought them flowers himself. He told the Pandya king Kulasekhara, to whom Shiva appeared in a dream in the form of a siddha and showed how the temple, its halls, walls and streets were to be laid out. When the city was built Shiva sprinkled over it nectar from the moon he wears, carried in the Ganga of his hair, and from that sweetness the city took the name Madurai (padalam 3).

The king's son Malayadhvaja married Kanchanamalai, but they had no child. He performed a sacrifice for offspring, and from the fire rose a girl who came and sat in the queen's lap. She had three breasts, and the king grieved until a voice from the sky told him that the third would vanish when she met the man she was to marry. She was named Tadatakai, trained in arms as well as learning, and, since there was no son, crowned to rule the Pandya land, which she governed with justice (padalam 4). The same padalam explains her birth through an earlier life: Kanchanamalai had been Vidyavati, a gandharva woman who fasted and sang before the Goddess at Madurai and asked that the Goddess be born as her daughter.

When her mother worried that she was not yet married, Tadatakai set out to conquer the four quarters. She defeated the kings of the north, Indra gave way before her, and she reached Kailasa, where Shiva's attendants fled. When Shiva himself came out, her third breast vanished; shyness came over her, and she stood with her fish-like eyes lowered. Her minister Sumati told her that this was her bridegroom. Shiva promised to come to Madurai to marry her on a Monday eight days later, and he came on his bull, attended by Vishnu and Brahma; Kanchanamalai washed his feet, and the wedding was celebrated before gods, kings and people. Shiva then ruled Madurai himself as the Pandya king, his bull banner becoming the fish banner (padalam 5).

The Thiruppugazh uses this as the Goddess's Madurai name. Song 708 recalls that Murugan, coming as the child poet Thirugnanasambandar, sang of Shiva who rules Madurai with his consort Angayarkanni, "the lady of beautiful eyes", the name by which the Goddess of Madurai is known in Tamil beside Meenakshi.
