When Ravana had fallen and Sita had been restored, the fourteen years of Rama's exile were nearly over. Vibhishana, now king of Lanka, placed at Rama's disposal the Pushpaka, the aerial chariot that Ravana had once taken from Kubera. Rama, Sita and Lakshmana went up into it together with Vibhishana and the leaders of the monkey army. Kamban describes Rama boarding the chariot with a glad mind, thinking that their task was now complete, while the gods rained flowers and called out their blessings (Kamba Ramayanam, Mitchip Padalam 4).

On the way north Rama pointed out to Sita the places of their long journey, and he sent Hanuman ahead to bring word to Bharata. Bharata had lived throughout the exile in the village of Nandigrama outside Ayodhya, dressed as an ascetic and governing only in Rama's name, with Rama's sandals set upon the throne. The brothers were reunited, and Bharata returned the kingdom he had held in trust.

The coronation itself is told at length in the last chapters of Valmiki's Yuddha Kanda. Rama was consecrated king by the sage Vasishtha and the priests, with Sita at his side. In the same account Rama gives Sita a necklace of pearls, and at his sign she places it on Hanuman (Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda 128). Kamban tells how the royal preceptor and the masters of ritual, with Vasishtha, fixed the day and hour at which the son of Dasaratha would wear the crown, and how, at the summons of the messengers, all the three worlds gathered at Ayodhya (Thirumudi Soottu Padalam 11–12). The lamps with which many households still remember the homecoming belong to later tradition rather than to these texts.

The Azhwars loved this ending. Kulasekhara Azhwar describes the return to Ayodhya and the coronation in the city of tall jewelled mansions (Perumal Thirumozhi 10.8), and Periyazhwar recalls the return in the Pushpaka with Sita, the crowning, and the long reign that followed (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 4.9.1).

In the Thiruppugazh the return is named once, at the close of a line that gathers up the whole war. In song 959, at Madurai, Arunagiri calls Murugan the beloved nephew of Hari, who cut down the thieving demon who had wronged the praised Janaki and then came back to victorious Ayodhya. For Arunagiri the homecoming is the seal on the victory: the Lord whose nephew he sings returns to his own city as its king.
