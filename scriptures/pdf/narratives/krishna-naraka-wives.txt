The story of Naraka is told in the fifty-ninth chapter of the tenth book of the Bhagavata Purana. Naraka, also called Bhauma, the son of the earth goddess, had stolen the earrings of Aditi, the mother of the gods, together with the umbrella of Varuna and a peak of Mount Mandara. Indra came to Krishna and told him, and Krishna rode on Garuda, taking his queen Satyabhama with him, to Naraka's city of Pragjyotisha, which was guarded by hills, weapons, water, fire and wind and a mesh of sharp cables (Bhagavata Purana 10.59.2–3). He broke through each defence with club, arrows, discus and sword, and the sound of his conch shattered the seals of the fortress (10.59.4–5). The five-headed demon Mura rose from the moat and was slain (10.59.6–11), Naraka's army was destroyed, and when Naraka raised his trident against the Lord, Krishna cut off his head with the discus (10.59.21).

The earth goddess then came to Krishna, returned Aditi's earrings and the other stolen treasures, and praised him; she asked him to protect Naraka's frightened son, and he did (10.59.23–32). Entering the palace, Krishna found sixteen thousand royal maidens whom Naraka had carried off by force from many kings (10.59.33). The Bhagavata says that when they saw him, each of them in her heart accepted him as her husband (10.59.34–35). He had them dressed in clean garments and sent in palanquins to Dwaraka with chariots, horses and treasure (10.59.36), and later married them all, each in her own palace (10.59.42). The Bhagavata does not dwell on what would have become of them otherwise; the common explanation, that women held by Naraka would have had no place in society and that Krishna gave them honour and a home, belongs to the tradition around the text.

The Alvars tell the story with the same emphasis on rescue. Periyazhwar speaks of Naraka, sure that none could kill him, holding the celestial maidens captive until Krishna killed him (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 4.3.3), and asks whether this is how the Lord protected the sixteen thousand women who had no refuge (4.3.6); elsewhere he pictures the sixteen thousand consorts serving him in Dwaraka (4.9.4). Thirumangai Alvar remembers that he destroyed the victorious Naraka with his discus (Periya Thirumozhi 3.10.2).

Arunagiri's one reference is brief and comes from the end of the story rather than the battle. Song 442, sung at Thiruvannamalai, calls Murugan the nephew of the emerald-coloured Narayana, the handsome Lord of the northern cowherds whose shoulders the sixteen thousand women embraced. Naraka himself is not named in the song.
