The anointing of Yudhishthira as king closes the long story of the Pandavas' struggle for their kingdom. It comes in the Shanti Parva of the Mahabharata, after the war at Kurukshetra and after the funeral rites for the fallen. The opening chapters of that book describe Yudhishthira's grief: he wished to renounce the kingdom won at such cost, and his brothers, Draupadi, the sages and Krishna argued with him at length before he agreed to rule. They are given here only in outline.

The Critical Edition describes the scene with care. Yudhishthira, his grief and fever gone, sat on a golden seat facing east, and facing him sat Satyaki and Vasudeva (Krishna), with Bhima and Arjuna on either side of the king (Mahabharata 12.40.1–3). Kunti sat with Sahadeva and Nakula; Vidura, Dhaumya and Dhritarashtra were there, and Yuyutsu, Sanjaya and Gandhari sat near the old king (12.40.4–6). Yudhishthira touched white flowers, auspicious marks, unhusked grain, earth, gold, silver and gems, and the people of the kingdom came with the family priest bringing the materials for the consecration (12.40.7–11). With the permission of Krishna, the priest Dhaumya prepared the altar, seated the king and Draupadi, and offered oblations into the fire (12.40.12–14). Then the lord of the earth, Kunti's son Yudhishthira, was anointed, and with him the royal sage Dhritarashtra and all the people joined in the anointing (12.40.15). Several manuscripts add that Krishna rose, took up his honoured conch and anointed the king with it, and that Yudhishthira, consecrated with the Panchajanya, shone; these lines are not part of the constituted text of the Critical Edition but show how the tradition placed Krishna at the centre of the rite. Drums sounded, the brahmins blessed the king and congratulated him on his victory, and he accepted the great kingdom with his friends (12.40.16–22).

Arunagiri refers to this outcome in two songs, crediting it to Krishna. Song 573, sung at Viralimalai, speaks of the Lord who brought ruin on the hundred Kauravas and set Kunti's sons to rule the seven worlds firmly and justly, the same Lord who had taken the form of the dwarf. Song 155, sung at Palani, appears to speak of the one who drove the chariot and gave the Kuru land to the five brothers, though the reading of that line is uncertain. In both, the coronation is not a story told for itself but the fruit of Krishna's friendship with the Pandavas.
