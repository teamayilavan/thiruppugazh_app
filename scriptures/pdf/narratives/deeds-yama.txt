The story of Markandeya is one of the best loved of all the deeds of Shiva. As it is commonly told, the boy Markandeya was fated to live only to the age of sixteen. He spent his short life in the worship of Shiva, and when the appointed day came and Yama, the lord of death, arrived to take him, the boy clung to the linga. Yama cast his noose, and it fell around the linga as well as the boy. Shiva came out of the linga, struck Yama down with his foot, and gave Markandeya lasting youth. For this deed Shiva is called Kalantaka or Kalasamharamurti, the one who ends death.

The Tamil hymns of the Tevaram return to the story often, and above all at Thirukkadavur (Kadavur Virattam). Appar sings that for the boy Markandeya the Lord kicked the fierce Yama, whose tongue lolled and whose fangs showed (Tevaram 4.107.1), and that the boy worshipped chanting the five sacred letters (4.107.2). In another hymn at Kadavur he says that Markandeya worshipped with milk, curd and ghee (4.31.9); at Chaikkadu, that he worshipped with flowers, and that when Yama came on the appointed day the Lord struck him down (4.65.1); and at Valanchuli, that the boy brought water and flowers (5.66.4). Sundarar sings of it at Mullaivayil (7.69.9), and Sambandar at Venkadu and Tiruvarur (2.61.7; 2.101.3).

The deed is counted among the eight heroic acts of Shiva at the eight Virattam shrines, and Tirumular, in the Tirumandiram, reads the subduing of Yama at Kadavur inwardly, as the mastery of the breath (Tirumandiram, second Tantra, Virattam section, verse 7). Manikkavasagar lists the kicking of Yama among the Lord's deeds, beside the taking of Brahma's head and the burning of the three cities (Tiruvasagam, Tiruporchunnam 18).

The hymns cited here dwell on the worship and the kick. The exact age of sixteen, the noose falling around the linga, and the gift of youth that lasts for ever belong to the story as it is generally told in the tradition.

In the Thiruppugazh, Arunagiri recalls the deed in a few strong words, usually in a list of Shiva's acts. In song 1106 the Lord kicks Yama in the chest; in songs 1135, 1160 and 1176 the kicking of Yama stands beside the burning of Kama and the swallowing of the poison. Song 214, at Swamimalai, speaks of the feet that took the life of Yama, and song 1330, at Thiruppoovanam, calls Murugan the son of the Lord who beat Yama down. For the poet, who often prays to be saved from death's messengers, the story is a promise: the father of Murugan has already defeated death.
