The Linga Purana tells that Rudra once went into the Daruka forest in the guise of a wanderer, naked, dark and handsome, to test the faith of the sages who lived there and of their wives (Linga Purana 1.29). The sages were devoted to their rites; the stranger who came begging among them was not recognised. The Purana goes on to tell how the sages, who had failed to know him, came at last to repentance and worship. The nakedness of the wanderer is part of the meaning of the form: he owns nothing, and he is beyond the rules by which the sages measured holiness.

Later tradition adds two well-loved details. Vishnu is said to have come with Shiva in the form of the enchantress Mohini, so that the sages too were drawn from their rites. And Tamil temple tradition tells that the angry sages raised a fire against the stranger and sent from it a tiger, a serpent, a deer and a dwarf; Shiva wore the tiger's skin, put on the serpent as an ornament, held the deer, and danced with the dwarf beneath his foot. These details are tradition, not part of the Linga Purana passage, but they explain the familiar image of the Lord as Bhikshatana and as the dancer.

The Tamil hymns love this form and speak of it with tenderness rather than scandal. In his very first hymn Sambandar calls the Lord who goes for alms with a skull "the thief who stole my heart" (Tevaram 1.1.2, 1.1.4). At Tiruparithiniyamam he gives a whole hymn to the beggar who goes from house to house and steals the beauty of the women's glances, so that their bangles slip (Tevaram 3.104.1, 3.104.7). At Mayiladuthurai he again pictures the Lord walking among the houses for alms (Tevaram 3.70.5). The longing of the women in these hymns is the longing of the soul for God.

The skull that the beggar carries is, in the Shaiva texts, the skull of Brahma's fifth head, and Arunagiri names it so. In song 509 (Chidambaram) he speaks of the bowl made from Brahma's skull, and he recalls the wandering Lord in songs 702 (Madambakkam) and 1166. For Arunagiri, the beggar who humbled the proud sages is the father of Murugan, and the same lesson runs through his own songs: rites and learning without love do not reach the Lord.
