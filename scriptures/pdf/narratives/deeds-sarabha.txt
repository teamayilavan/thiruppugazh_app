In the Bhagavata Purana and in the Vaishnava tradition generally, Vishnu takes the form of Narasimha, half man and half lion, to destroy the demon king Hiranyakashipu and protect his son, the devotee Prahlada. Certain Shaiva Puranas continue the story beyond that point. They tell that after the demon's death the fury of Narasimha did not cool, so that the worlds were troubled, and that Shiva took a terrible form called Sarabha in order to bring it to rest. The story belongs to these later texts and to Shaiva tradition, not to the Bhagavata, and it is told here with that in mind.

The Linga Purana devotes two chapters to it. The first recounts the deeds of Narasimha (Linga Purana 1.95); the second tells how Shiva took the fierce Sarabha form to curb him, and how Narasimha then praised Shiva in a hymn (Linga Purana 1.96). The Shiva Purana tells the story in its account of Shiva's incarnations, where the Sarabha form appears after a prologue (Shiva Purana, Satarudra-samhita, chapters 11–12). In that telling Shiva first sends Virabhadra to speak to Narasimha, and takes the Sarabha form only when gentle words fail. Sarabha is pictured as a wondrous creature, part lion and part great bird. The details of the encounter vary between the texts, and different communities of devotees hold the two great gods sacred in their own ways. The heart of the Shaiva telling is that the fury which saved the world had itself to be quieted, so that the world could be at peace again.

The story is not among those the Tevaram saints sing of again and again, and it is rare in the Thiruppugazh as well. Arunagiri touches it in two songs. In song 424, sung at Tiruvannamalai, he names the Narasimha who sprang from the pillar and the Lord who took the fierce form of the Sarabha bird, and he praises the Goddess as the faithful Lady who shares that Lord's side. In song 448, sung at Thirukkalathi (Kalahasti), he again recalls Shankara with Uma on his left in connection with the Sarabha form. In both songs the reference is brief and comes in praise of Parvati, the mother of Murugan, rather than as a story told for its own sake.
