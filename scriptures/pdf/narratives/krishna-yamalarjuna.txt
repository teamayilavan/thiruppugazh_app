The breaking of the twin arjuna trees follows directly on the binding of Krishna to the mortar, and the Bhagavata Purana tells the two as a single story. While Yashoda was busy with her housework, the child, still tied to the mortar, noticed two arjuna trees in the yard which had once been Nalakuvara and Manigriva, the sons of Kubera, cursed by the sage Narada for their pride (Bhagavata Purana 10.9.22–23).

The next chapter explains the curse. The two brothers, proud of their wealth and of their place among Shiva's companions, were sporting drunk and naked with young women in the Mandakini river near Kailasa when Narada came by. The women covered themselves in shame, but the brothers did not care (10.10.2–6). Narada, the Bhagavata says, wished to show them mercy through a curse. After a long reflection on how wealth blinds people, he declared that since they behaved like trees, trees they should be, keeping their memory, until they met Vasudeva himself (10.10.7–22). He went away, and they became the twin arjuna trees (10.10.23).

Krishna, wishing to make the words of his devotee Narada come true, crawled between the two trees. He passed through, but the mortar turned crosswise and stuck between them, and when he pulled it with force the trees were uprooted and fell with a great crash (10.10.24–27). From them came two perfect beings shining like fire, who bowed and praised him (10.10.28). Krishna told them that Narada's curse had been a great favour, and sent them home; they walked around him, bound as he still was to the mortar, and left (10.10.40–43). The cowherds who came running at the noise could not believe that the child had done it, though the children insisted they had seen it, and Nanda smiled and untied his son (10.11.1–6).

The Tamil tradition gives the story a sharper edge. Periyazhwar sings of the child who snapped the twin maruda trees (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 3.9.9), but elsewhere speaks of deceiving demons who stood as twin trees and were destroyed (3.1.3), and Thirumangai Alvar ranks the trees with Putana and the cart among the Lord's enemies (Periya Thirumozhi 8.6.8). In the Bhagavata, by contrast, the trees are cursed gods awaiting release.

Arunagiri mentions the maruda trees in at least nine songs. Song 908, sung at Vayalur, captures the sound of the trees cracking as the Lord crawls with the rolling mortar. Song 851 sets the trees beside the death of Kamsa, and song 576 at Viralimalai names Mukunda who kicked down the maruda. Song 116 at Palani lists the trees with the cart and the lifted hill among the deeds of Murugan's uncle.
