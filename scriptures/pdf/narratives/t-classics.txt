Some songs of the Thiruppugazh do not tell a story at all; they name books. In a handful of songs Arunagiri lists the classics of Tamil learning, the Tirukkural, the poems of the Sangam age, the Tevaram and others, and he names them in order to make a point about learning that is only for show.

Song 76, sung at Thiruchendur, describes people who approach mean and treacherous rich men and praise them as a great sun, hoping for a gift. These people have studied the flawless works on ethics and the noted literature of the Sangam, histories, stories, epics and the sixty-four arts; they have chanted and taken in the ancient work of Tiruvalluvar, the Tirukkural; and they turn out garlands of verse in many forms. Yet all of it is spent on flattery. In song 60, also sung at Thiruchendur, the poet speaks of himself: he has neglected worship, and has spent his days researching the rare texts of the three Sangam ages and speaking sweetly to deceivers, and he asks to be kept away from them.

Song 171, sung at Palani, describes proud men with rings on their fingers and long earrings, who move about with an air of learning; but if someone asks them to explain a poem, or a stanza from an old text like the Tirukkural, their faces turn pale. The poet asks Murugan to put an end to such learning. Song 104, also of Palani, makes a comparison of a different kind: for removing suffering, it says, the Tevaram is far greater than the Tirukkural, which treats of virtue, wealth and love. The same song recalls the tradition that Murugan, as Thirugnanasambandar, wrote a Tevaram hymn on a palm leaf that floated upstream in the Vaigai.

The works the songs name are real and well known. The Tirukkural has one hundred and thirty-three chapters and one thousand three hundred and thirty couplets. The Sangam poems survive in collections such as the Purananuru, in which, for example, the poet Kapilar praises the chieftain Pari (Purananuru 105 to 111). But the songs quote no lines from any of them. They name the works as a whole, as a listener of the time would have known them.

Arunagiri is not against learning; he knew the old books well, and his own verse is full of them. What he sets aside is learning without devotion, learning used to win a rich man's favour or to make a show. Against it he sets the grace of Murugan, and, in song 104, the hymns of the Tevaram. Songs 60, 76, 104 and 171 together give his judgment: the names of great books are no substitute for the Lord.
