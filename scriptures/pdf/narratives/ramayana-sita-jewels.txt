When Ravana carried Sita off through the sky toward Lanka, she passed over a mountain on which five monkeys were sitting. Valmiki tells that Sita, hoping they might one day tell Rama what had happened, tied some of her ornaments in her silken upper cloth and let them fall among them, unseen by Ravana. Ravana, intent on his flight, did not notice, and carried her on to Lanka.

The monkeys were Sugriva, then living in exile from his brother Vali, and his companions, among them Hanuman. They kept what had fallen. Long afterwards, when Rama and Lakshmana came to the hills of Kishkindha searching for Sita and made friends with Sugriva, Sugriva told Rama that he had seen a lady carried through the sky crying out the names of Rama and Lakshmana, and he brought out the cloth and the ornaments she had dropped (Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 6.11–20). Rama recognised them, pressed them to his heart and wept. In the same chapter Lakshmana, asked to look at them, says that he does not know the armlets or the earrings, but that he knows the anklets, because he bowed every day at Sita's feet. The scene has become one of the best-loved pictures of Lakshmana's reverence for Sita.

The ornaments matter to the plot as the first sure sign of Sita's path. They confirm to Rama that she was carried south and that Sugriva's people had seen her, and they bind the new friendship between Rama and Sugriva that leads to the death of Vali, the coronation of Sugriva and the great search.

The link to the Thiruppugazh is uncertain. One song, 1325 at Punavayil, has been connected with this episode: its closing lines call Murugan the beloved nephew of Narayana, and speak of a ball of gems thrown and of mountains strung like a chain around one who saw the whole sky. Those images may as well belong to Vishnu's measuring of the worlds as to Sita's jewels, and the reading here is offered with caution. Arunagiri does not otherwise tell the story; it stands in the Ramayana as a quiet sign of hope between the loss of Sita and her finding.
