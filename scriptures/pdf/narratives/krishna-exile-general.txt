The Pandavas' exile is the long hinge of the Mahabharata, between the dice game and the war. In the Sabha Parva, Yudhishthira, challenged to play dice by Duryodhana with his uncle Shakuni throwing for him, loses his wealth, his kingdom, his brothers, himself and Draupadi. The final wager sets the terms of the exile: the losers are to live twelve years in the forest and a thirteenth year unrecognised, and if they are discovered in that year the term begins again.

The Vana Parva, the third and one of the longest books of the epic, covers the twelve forest years. The brothers and Draupadi live in the forests of Kamyaka and Dvaita, visited by sages who tell them many of the old stories the epic preserves; Krishna also comes to see them there. The Virata Parva then tells of the thirteenth year, which they spend in disguise as servants at the court of King Virata. When the years are over they ask for their kingdom back. Duryodhana refuses, and even Krishna's embassy to the Kuru court in the Udyoga Parva cannot move him, so that the war at Kurukshetra follows.

This book does not retell the many episodes of the forest years one by one; several of them, and the war that follows, appear as stories of their own. What matters here is the shape of the whole: the Pandavas keep their word to the last day of a hard bargain, and are refused all the same.

Arunagiri refers to the exile once, in song 785 (Thirukkadavur). There he recalls how, as fate decreed, the Kuru princes went to the forest, lived in disguise counting the days until the term was complete, and how the great hero, the Mayan, then brought about a Bharata war; Murugan is praised as the nephew of that Mayan. The lines compress the whole sequence of exile, hidden year and war into a single breath, as the Thiruppugazh often does with the epics, and name Krishna as the one who guided it to its end.
