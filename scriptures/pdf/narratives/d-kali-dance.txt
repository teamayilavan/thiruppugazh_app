Thiruvalangadu, the "forest of banyan trees" near Thiruvallur, is one of the shrines where Shiva is worshipped as the dancer. Its temple legend tells of a dance contest between Shiva and Kali. The tellings differ in their details, but the outline is this. Kali, after destroying the demons, could not be calmed, and in her fury she came to the banyan forest and threatened the place where Shiva dwelt. Rather than fight her, Shiva proposed a contest in dance. She matched him in every pose until he lifted one foot straight up to his ear and held it there, the pose known as the Urdhva Tandava. Kali would not take that pose, and she yielded. Some accounts give the number of poses she matched, some say that an earring or a gem from an anklet fell and that Shiva lifted it back with his foot, and they disagree on which leg was raised. The contest is a temple legend and should be read as such.

The older Tamil hymns of the shrine know Shiva as a dancer there, but they do not tell the contest. Karaikkal Ammaiyar's Mootha Tirupathigam, the "elder sacred decad" of the eleventh Tirumurai, describes Shiva dancing in the burning ground of Alangadu, among howling ghosts and leaping fire, with his matted hair flung out to the eight directions (Tirumurai 11.2, verses 1 and 7). Sambandar's hymn at Thiruvalangadu (Tevaram 1.45) likewise praises the Lord of the place without describing a contest with Kali.

It is in the Thiruppugazh that the contest is named. In song 94, sung at Thiruchendur, Arunagiri describes the Goddess who shares the left half of the body of the great dancer, Shiva who rides the bull, and says that as Kali she danced in challenge against him; the same lines call her the Mother whom devotees come to worship every day. Song 117, sung at Palani, names her as Kali who contended with the Lord, the daughter of the mountain, and the sister of Vishnu, and says that she gave birth to Murugan.

For Arunagiri, then, the Kali of the contest is not another being opposed to Shiva but Parvati herself, his other half and Murugan's mother, in her fiercest form. The dance ends not in defeat but in peace, and the songs that recall it are 94 (Thiruchendur) and 117 (Palani).
