At the climax of the war, Rama faced Ravana alone, chariot against chariot, Rama now driven by Matali in the chariot that Indra had sent. Valmiki describes a strange and frightening turn in the duel: Rama cuts off one of Ravana's heads with an arrow, but another rises in its place; he cuts again, and again the head grows back, until a hundred heads of equal splendour have been cut off with no end in sight (Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha 107.54–58). Rama, who had killed Maricha, Khara and Vali with these arrows, wonders why they now fail. It is then that Matali reminds him of the weapon of Brahma, with which Ravana is finally killed.

Kamban tells the same struggle with great intensity. Rama sees the moment and, with a single crescent-headed arrow, strikes off one head (Kamba Ramayanam, Ravanan Vadhai Padalam 148). It falls into the sea, Kamban says, like a peak of Mount Meru broken off in a storm (149). But the head, as if born again by the merit of dharma, rises in its place, and the poet comments that there is nothing that penance cannot do (151). The duel becomes ceaseless: as one head becomes a hundred, Rama looses a thousand arrows (161), and the fallen heads lie on every wave, every land, every mountain and in every direction, like falling thunderbolts (162).

The Alvars, too, remember the heads that grew back. Periyazhwar sings of Ravana's ten heads, which regrow by his boon, being cut together, and of their final severing with the weapon of Brahma (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 2.6.8 and 3.9.10). Kulasekhara Alvar, singing as Kausalya, calls the infant Rama the one who scattered the ten heads of the lord of Lanka with his arrows (Perumal Thirumozhi 8.1), and Thirumangai Alvar speaks of Rama severing Ravana's twenty strong shoulders with his bow (Periya Thirumozhi 5.1.4).

No episode of the Ramayana appears more often in the Thiruppugazh: some fifty songs refer to it. Arunagiri seldom dwells on the regrowth itself. His usual image is of Ravana's ten heads, or ten crowns, falling to the Lord's arrows, often to a single arrow. In song 29 (Thiruchendur) Raghava, having bridged the dark sea, looses one arrow at the many crowned heads of the fearless Ravana. In song 1007 (a general song) the ten crowns and twenty arms fall together to a single arrow. In song 166 (Palani) the Lord bridges the sea, cuts off the jewelled crowns of the terrible Ravana and takes Janaki back, and Murugan is the nephew who calls this Mayan his uncle.
