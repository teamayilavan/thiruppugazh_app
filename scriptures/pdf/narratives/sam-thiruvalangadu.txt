Thiruvalangadu, "the banyan forest", near the old village of Pazhaiyanur in the northern Tamil country, is one of the shrines Sambandar visited on his journey through the northern Tamil country. The Periya Puranam tells the visit in a few verses. Having worshipped at the shrines of the region, Sambandar came near Pazhaiyanur and Thiruvalangadu (Periya Puranam, Sambandar Puranam 1007). He would not set foot in the town, because it was the place where "our Mother", Karaikkal Ammaiyar, had come walking on her head in reverence; fearing to tread there, he spent the night in a neighbouring village (v. 1008).

In the middle of the night, the Puranam says, the Lord of the banyan forest appeared to him in a dream and asked, "Have you forgotten to sing of us?" (v. 1009). The child woke at once, praised the compassion of the Lord who drank the poison of the ocean, and sang a hymn beginning "Thunja Varuvarum", in which he praised the people of Pazhaiyanur for keeping their word (v. 1010). In the morning he told the gathered devotees what the Lord had said, sang again, and went on toward Thiruppasur (vv. 1011–1012). The hymn is preserved as Tevaram 1.45, a decad of twelve verses; its first verse names Alangadu and recalls the story of Neeli at Pazhaiyanur. According to the local tradition, the Vellalas of Pazhaiyanur gave their lives rather than break a promise of protection, which the ghost Neeli had tricked them into; the hymn alludes to this, but does not tell the story in full.

Thiruvalangadu is also remembered as one of the five halls of Shiva's dance, and its temple tradition tells of a dance before the gods, and of a contest of dance between Shiva and Kali. Neither Sambandar's hymn nor the verses of the Puranam read here describe that dance. It is the temple's own tradition, told in its own story (see the Devi set), and the film presents it as such.

Arunagiri refers to Sambandar at Thiruvalangadu in one song. Song 674, sung at Thiruvalangadu itself, says that Murugan came as Sambandar to this place where Shiva danced for the celestials. The song joins two things that the older sources keep apart, the saint's visit and the temple's dance, into a single image: the son coming, as a child singer, to the place where his father danced.
