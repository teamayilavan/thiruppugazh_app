The descent of the Ganga is told in the first book of the Ramayana, where the sage Vishvamitra recounts it to the young Rama. The sixty thousand sons of King Sagara had been burned to ashes, and only the waters of the heavenly Ganga could purify them and win them heaven. Their descendant Bhagiratha undertook a long penance; Brahma granted that the river should descend, but warned that the earth could not bear the force of her fall, and that only Shiva could receive it.

Bhagiratha then worshipped Shiva for a year, standing on the tip of one toe, and Shiva agreed to bear the river on his head. Ganga, in her pride, thought she would carry Shiva away with her into the underworld. Knowing her mind, Shiva held her in the coils of his matted hair, where she wandered for many years and could not find her way out. When Bhagiratha prayed again, Shiva released her into the Bindu lake, from which she flowed in seven streams, one of them following Bhagiratha's chariot (Ramayana 1.43.1–10). The Ramayana goes on to tell how she reached the ashes of Sagara's sons and how they rose to heaven. The Bhagavata Purana tells the same story briefly: Bhagiratha pleased Shiva, who agreed to bear the Ganga on his head (Bhagavata Purana 9.9.8–9), and the Mahabharata tells it in the Vana Parva (Mahabharata 3,107–108).

In the Tamil hymns, the river in Shiva's hair is one of his best-loved marks. Sambandar sings that the Lord favoured the penance of Bhagiratha, hid the Ganga in his matted hair, and brought her gently down to earth (Tevaram 3.122.3). In other hymns he says the Lord hid the surging river in a single lock (Tevaram 1.39.8, 1.105.6), in his fragrant hair (Tevaram 1.118.8), or there beside the crescent moon (Tevaram 1.104.7, 2.67.1). The image is less of a struggle than of a great gentleness: the force that could have broken the world is held and softened.

Arunagiri mentions the Ganga in Shiva's hair in some thirty songs, almost always as part of a name for Murugan's father: the Lord who wears the river and the moon in his matted locks. Examples are songs 32, 52 and 77 (Thiruchendur), 199 (Palani), 519 (Kailasa) and 652 (Kasi). In these songs the river in the hair is one of the ornaments by which the father is recognised, and through him the son.
