The name Matangi occurs once in the Thiruppugazh, in song 94, which Arunagiri sang at Thiruchendur. In a long passage of praise addressed to the Goddess, he calls her "the one who ate the dark poison, Matangi, of whom the Vedas speak". The commentary explains Matangi as the daughter of the sage Matanga, the form in which the Goddess was born to him.

This is all that the songs say about Matangi. In Shakta tradition she is honoured as one of the forms of the Goddess, but the Thiruppugazh does not tell the story behind the name, and this book does not supply one. The film made from this passage is therefore an invocation rather than a narrative. It shows a sage in a forest hermitage and a light that becomes the Goddess, and it says plainly that the song does not tell how she came to him.

The name is best understood in the company in which Arunagiri places it. In the same lines of song 94 the Goddess is the one who drank the primal poison, a deed the songs usually give to Shiva, here shared by his consort who is his other half; she is praised by the Vedas; she is the great Durga; and, as Kali, she is the one who with her feet drove back Yama, the god of death, when he came to take the life of the young Markandeya. A little further on the same song describes her as the one who shares the left half of the body of Shiva, the great dancer who rides the bull, and who, as Bhadra Kali, danced in fierce challenge against him; and it calls her the Mother whom devotees come to worship every day.

Seen this way, Matangi is one name in a garland of names. Arunagiri does not stop to tell the story of each; he heaps them up, one after another, to show that the many forms of the Goddess, gentle and fierce, ascetic and royal, are one Mother. The garland leads, as his songs always do, to Murugan, who is her son.

The only song to cite is 94 (Thiruchendur), the same song that recalls Kali's dance in challenge with Shiva.
