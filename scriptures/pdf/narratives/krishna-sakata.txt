The overturning of the cart is one of the earliest wonders of Krishna's infancy in Gokula. The Bhagavata Purana sets it at the festival Yashoda held when the baby, about three months old, first tried to turn over by himself. After bathing him and honouring the brahmins, she laid the sleepy child down, and while she was busy receiving guests she did not hear him crying for milk. Hungry and cross, the baby kicked his legs upward (Bhagavata Purana 10.7.4–6).

He was lying under a handcart in a corner of the courtyard, and although his legs were as soft as leaves, when they struck the cart it turned over and collapsed: the wheels came off the axle, the hubs and spokes fell apart, the pole broke, and the metal vessels on it were scattered (10.7.7). The grown-ups could find no cause and wondered whether a demon or an evil planet was at work. The small children said the baby had kicked the cart, but the adults dismissed this as childish talk (10.7.8–10). Yashoda, fearing that some evil influence had attacked her son, nursed him and had the brahmins perform rites for his protection, while the cowherd men set the cart upright again (10.7.11–12).

In the Bhagavata's account there is no demon in the cart; the wonder lies in the baby's strength and in the adults' disbelief. The Tamil tradition tells it differently. For the Alvars the cart was a demon in disguise, and the infant kicked it to pieces. Periyazhwar speaks of the "deceitful cart" (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 3.9.9), Andal praises the one who kicked the cart in her litany of the Lord's deeds (Tiruppavai 24), and Thirumangai Alvar names the Lord who destroyed Sakata with his feet, with Putana and the twin trees (Periya Thirumozhi 8.6.8). The name Sakatasura, the cart demon, belongs to this wider tradition.

Arunagiri follows the Tamil reading. In song 1157 the Lord who grew up in the cowherd village kicked the strong cart with his foot, and in songs 116 and 186, both sung at Palani, the kicking of the cart stands beside the breaking of the maruda trees and the lifting of the hill as the deeds of Murugan's uncle. The tiny foot that overturns the heavy cart becomes, in these lines, one more sign of the Lord's hidden greatness.
