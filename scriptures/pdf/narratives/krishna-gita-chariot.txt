The Bhagavad Gita opens in the Bhishma Parva of the Mahabharata, on the first day of the war, with the words "On the field of dharma, on the field of the Kurus" (Mahabharata, Bhishma Parva 6.23.1; Bhagavad Gita 1.1). The two armies stand ready. Bhishma blows his conch, and on the Pandava side Krishna blows Panchajanya and Arjuna Devadatta (6.23.15). Arjuna then asks Krishna, who is driving his chariot, to halt it between the two armies so that he may see whom he has to fight (6.23.21), and Krishna does so, stopping the chariot before Bhishma and Drona and telling him to look at the assembled Kurus (6.23.24–25). Seeing grandfathers, teachers, uncles, brothers, sons and friends on both sides, Arjuna is overcome; he lets his bow fall and sits down in the chariot (6.23.47).

What Krishna then teaches him, through the eighteen chapters of the Gita, is not retold here. At its close Arjuna says that his delusion is destroyed, that he stands firm with his doubts gone, and that he will do as Krishna says (Bhagavad Gita 18.73), and the war begins. Krishna remains his charioteer to the end.

The image of the Lord as Partha's charioteer, the reins and whip in his hands, is beloved in the Tamil country. At Thiruvallikkeni Thirumangai Alvar sings of the one who stood before Arjuna's chariot as its driver, with the stick in his hand (Periya Thirumozhi 2.3.1), and elsewhere of the Lord who stayed on Arjuna's chariot as its driver while great warriors despaired (7.5.2).

Arunagiri uses the image in about eleven songs, more than any other scene of the Mahabharata. In song 339 (Kanchipuram) he is the Achyuta who drove the shining chariot so that the kshatriyas fell in eighteen days; in song 527 (Thiruvenkatam) the tall Lord who drove the Pandavas' chariot; in song 574 (Viralimalai) Vijaya's great charioteer; in song 928 (Karuvur) the one who urged on the horses of Arjuna's chariot; and in song 1053 he blows the conch from the chariot on which Vijaya rides. Each time Murugan is praised as the nephew of the divine charioteer.
