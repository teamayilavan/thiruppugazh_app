Once Hanuman had returned from Lanka with news of Sita, Rama, Sugriva and the gathered hosts of monkeys and bears set out from Kishkindha for the southern sea. Valmiki describes the army moving south in high spirits, eager for Sita's release, roaring and leaping as it went, guarded on every side by its captains (Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha 4.62–70). In his telling Hanuman carries Rama on his shoulders and Angada carries Lakshmana, and the army travels through forests and over mountains, living on the fruits and water it finds along the way.

Kamban spreads the march across two sections of his Tamil Ramayana. In the Thanai Kaan Padalam the commanders arrive in Kishkindha with their vast forces (Kamba Ramayanam, Thanai Kaan Padalam 1). One of these, Kesari, the father of Hanuman, comes with a host of monkeys like great mountains, numbered beyond counting, and moves like the sea itself (Thanai Kaan Padalam 5). Then, at the beginning of the Yuddha Kandam, Kamban brings the army to the shore: the great host spreads over the land and fills the coast (Kadal Kaan Padalam 1). Rama, parted from Sita, looks out at the sea, and Kamban sets beside him the lotuses closing in sleep (Kadal Kaan Padalam 2).

The episode is not a battle but a journey, and it has a joyful character. The monkeys and bears are ordinary forest creatures who have taken up a cause that is not their own, and they go to it gladly. The march ends where the next great difficulty begins, at the edge of the ocean that still separates Rama from Lanka.

Arunagiri recalls the march in two songs, each time in the line that praises Vishnu as Murugan's uncle. In song 941 (Pattaliyur) he speaks of the victorious Lord of the discus who could send the army of Sugriva on its way to Lanka. In song 1231 (a general song) he describes King Sugriva coming with his countless strong monkey army, going on so that the dust rose up over the sea, before Lanka was set on fire and the ten-headed king was killed by a single arrow.
