The burning of the three cities, Tripura, is one of the oldest stories told of Shiva. In the Mahabharata it is told in the Karna Parva as a model for Karna's chariot: three demon lords, Tarakaksha, Kamalaksha and Vidyunmali, held three cities that could not be destroyed, and the gods built for Shiva a chariot of the whole creation, with which he burned the cities with the Pashupata weapon (Mahabharata 8,24, especially 8,24.114–115). It is recalled in short allusions elsewhere in the epic (Mahabharata 7,173.37; 13,14.128), and the Ramayana says that the bow which Shiva used for the Tripura battle was given to him by the gods (Ramayana 1.75.12–16).

The Bhagavata Purana tells it through Narada: Maya, the architect of the demons, built three cities of gold, silver and iron; when Shiva's arrows struck them, Maya revived the dead in a well of nectar, and Vishnu intervened so that the cities could finally be burned (Bhagavata Purana 7.10.51–69). The Linga Purana describes the Tripura demons and the building of Rudra's chariot (Linga Purana 1.71–72), and the Shiva Purana tells of the three cities, of the virtue of their rulers, of how Vishnu turned them from it, and of the burning (Shiva Purana, Rudra-samhita, Yuddha-khanda, chapters 1, 3, 4 and 10).

The Tamil saints sang the story constantly. Sambandar describes the gods becoming the chariot, Mount Meru the bow, the serpent Vasuki the string and Vishnu the fire-tipped arrow (Tevaram 1.131.4); Appar gives nearly the same picture (Tevaram 6.19.1, 6.66.4), and in his hymn on Annamalai almost every verse returns to it (Tevaram 5.4.1, 5.4.9). Sundarar playfully asks why such a serpent and such a mountain were needed just to set three cities on fire (Tevaram 7.99.8). Manikkavasagar says no second arrow was seen in the Lord's hand, and tells of the chariot's axle snapping (Tiruvasagam, Tiruvundiyar, 14.1–3). Tamil tradition, recalled in Arunagiri's first song, explains the broken axle: Ganesha had not been honoured first. The tradition that Shiva burned the cities with a smile alone is likewise Tamil. Tirumular reads the three cities as the three impurities that bind the soul (Tirumandiram, second tantra, section 2, verse 5).

Arunagiri mentions the burning of the three cities in about sixty songs, more than any other deed of Shiva. He opens the whole Thiruppugazh with it: his invocation to Ganesha recalls the axle broken on the chariot of the Lord who burned the three cities (song 1). Other examples are songs 11 (Thiruparankundram), 52 and 71 (Thiruchendur), 124 (Palani) and 263 (Thiruthani).
