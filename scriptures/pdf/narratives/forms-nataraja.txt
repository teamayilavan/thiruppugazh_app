Nataraja, the Lord of dance, is the form of Shiva most closely tied to the Tamil country. At Chidambaram, the ancient Thillai, he dances in the Chitrambalam, the small hall whose roof is covered in gold, and his bronze image, with drum and flame, the raised foot and the ring of fire, has become one of the best known images of India.

The Sanskrit Puranas speak of Shiva as a dancer. The Linga Purana tells of his Tandava at dusk with ghosts and goblins, danced to please the Goddess (Linga Purana 1.106), and the Shiva Purana counts among his forms that of the dancing actor (Shiva Purana, Satarudra-samhita, chapter 34). But it is the Tamil hymns that give the dance its home. Appar, at Thillai, sings his famous verse: the curved brow, the red lips with their gentle smile, the matted hair, the white ash, and the golden foot raised in the dance; to see this, he says, is worth a human birth (Tevaram 4.81.4). Elsewhere he sees the Lord, with the Ganga in his hair and the Lady at his side, dancing in the blazing fire in the Chitrambalam (4.22.2). Manikkavasagar asks, in his playful question-and-answer verses, why the Lord of Thillai dances, and answers that if he did not, the world would be overrun by Kali's flesh-eating company (Tiruvasagam, Tiruchchazhal 14).

Beneath the dancing foot lies the dwarf Muyalakan. Appar sings of the Lord who keeps the unruly Muyalakan beneath his foot (Tevaram 6.96.3) and of the feet that quell his rage (4.100.7). Tirumular, in the Tirumandiram, reads the dwarf as the ego, and the dance as taking place in the great hall of space (ninth Tantra, Adbhuta Koothu, verse 13). The contest of dance with Kali at Thiruvalangadu, and the scene of Vishnu, Brahma and Adisesha watching, belong to the temple traditions of the dance.

Arunagiri mentions the dancing Lord in about forty-five songs, many of them sung at Chidambaram. He names the father who dances in the golden hall of Chidambaram (song 472), the Lord whose golden anklets ring as he dances with the deer and the axe in his hands (251), and the dance on Kailasa while Adisesha bowed his hoods (517). In song 479 Vishnu and Brahma cannot find his feet; in song 583 he dances in the burning ground. And in song 83 the poet joins the two great themes of his work: the dancer of Chidambaram's hall is the father into whose ear Murugan spoke the sacred words.
