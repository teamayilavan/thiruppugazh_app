The end of the Yadava clan, Krishna's own people, is told both in the Mausala Parva of the Mahabharata and at the opening of the eleventh book of the Bhagavata Purana. The Bhagavata frames it as the Lord's own design. Having removed the burden of the earth through the Kurukshetra war, Krishna reflected that the Yadavas, invincible to any outside force, were themselves still a burden, and resolved to withdraw his family on the pretext of a curse (Bhagavata Purana 11.1.1–5).

The sages Vishvamitra, Kanva, Durvasa, Narada and others went to the holy place of Pindaraka (11.1.11–12). There the young Yadavas brought Samba, Krishna's son by Jambavati, dressed as a pregnant woman, and mockingly asked the sages what the child would be. The angry sages replied that she would bear an iron club that would destroy the whole dynasty, and when the frightened boys uncovered Samba's belly, there was an iron club (11.1.13–17). They brought it to King Ugrasena, and Dwaraka was filled with fear (11.1.18–20). Ugrasena had the club ground to bits and threw them, with the remaining lump, into the sea (11.1.21). The bits were washed ashore and grew into tall, sharp reeds; a fish swallowed the lump, and the hunter Jara made it into an arrowhead (11.1.22–23). Krishna, though able to reverse the curse, did not wish to, and as Time itself sanctioned what followed (11.1.24).

The Mahabharata tells the same story with differences of detail. There the sages are at Dwaraka, the iron pestle is born to Samba the next day, and the king has it ground to powder and cast into the sea (Mahabharata 16.2.4–17); Krishna, knowing the end, says only that it shall be so and does not wish to change it (16.2.13–14). Years later, drunk at Prabhasa, the Yadavas fall to quarrelling. The Mahabharata says that a handful of eraka grass became "a terrible iron club, like a thunderbolt", and that the Andhakas, Bhojas and Vrishnis, "driven by Time", killed one another with these clubs (16.4.35–36). In that account Krishna himself, seeing his son and Satyaki slain, takes up a handful of the grass, which becomes a club in his hand (16.4.34–35). No Alvar verse on this episode was found.

Arunagiri's one reference is in song 248, sung at Thiruthanigai. In a list of Vishnu's deeds, after the slaying of Ravana and the binding of Mahabali, he names Murugan the nephew of the one who filed down the pestle and cast it into the middle of the sea. The texts give that act to Ugrasena; the song gives it to the Lord, in whose will, as the Bhagavata says, the whole event took place.
