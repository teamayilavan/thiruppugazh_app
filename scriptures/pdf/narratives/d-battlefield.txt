In the Thiruppugazh, Murugan's war against Suran and the demons is often heard rather than seen. Around the fighting Arunagiri gathers the Goddess's hosts, who dance and cry out in triumph, and an army of fiends who feast on the field. The picture is the poet's own, but it grows out of the war as the Kanda Puranam describes it.

The Puranam's Yuddha Kandam is full of Kali and her hosts. In one verse the armies are matched against each other, fiend against fiend and Kali against Kali (Yuddha Kandam 992); in another Kali and the fiends are seen among the slain at the height of a battle (1251). In a longer episode the Puranam even brings Kali, as Chandika, onto the field against Murugan's host: an asura prince who had worshipped her calls her to his aid, she rides out on her lion with an army of her own, is overcome by the warrior Murugan has sent, and then praises him, blesses him and withdraws (1394–1436). The joyful dance of the goddesses after Murugan's victory, and the kulavai cry, are not part of these verses.

They belong to the songs. Song 138 says that when Murugan's spear won, Bhadrakali, the form of Durga on the battlefield, raised the kulavai, the high, rolling cry that Tamil women still raise at weddings and festivals. Song 900 says Durga danced with a noise like the waves of a stormy sea, and Indra on his elephant called out in praise of Murugan as commander of the gods. Song 903 says Durga, who serves Uma, danced on the battlefield according to the rules of classical dance while the world chanted "Hara, Hara". Song 1079 describes the fiends of Durga gathering on either side of a dancer and beating their hand-drums in time, and song 1127 says that Durga and her hosts were delighted and the fiends had a feast when Murugan fought.

Arunagiri uses these images as a sign of victory. The dancing goddess, the cry of joy and the satisfied fiends show that the war is over and that the right side has won. The songs that use the image are 138 (Palani), 900 and 903 (Vayalur), and 1079 and 1127 among the general songs; the film keeps the dance and the cry, and turns the fiends' feast into comedy.
