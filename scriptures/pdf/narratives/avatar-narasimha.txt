The story of Narasimha is told in the seventh book of the Bhagavata Purana. Hiranyakashipu, king of the demons, learned from his son's teachers that the boy Prahlada was turning the other children toward devotion to Vishnu, and in his anger he resolved to kill him (Bhagavata Purana 7.8.1–5). He demanded to know by whose strength the boy defied him; Prahlada answered that the source of his strength was the source of his father's too, the one strength of all (7.8.6–8). Mocking him, the king asked where this Lord was: if he was everywhere, why was he not in this pillar? He drew his sword, rose from his throne and struck the pillar with his fist (7.8.12–14). The fuller tradition of the king's boon, that no man or beast could kill him by day or night, indoors or outdoors, and of his many attempts on the boy's life, is told in the earlier chapters of the same book and is followed here only as tradition.

From inside the pillar came a terrible sound that seemed to crack the covering of the universe; none of the demons could tell where it came from (7.8.15–16). To prove his servant's words true, that the Lord is present everywhere, even in a pillar of the hall, Hari appeared in a form neither man nor lion (7.8.17–18). The king attacked with his club and then with sword and shield, and the Lord seized him as Garuda seizes a snake, placed him on his lap at the doorway of the assembly hall, and tore him with his nails (7.8.25–29). The angry Lord then sat on the king's throne, and none dared approach him (7.8.34; 7.9.1–2), until the boy Prahlada went forward and bowed, and the Lord placed his hand on his head (7.9.3–5).

Kamban tells the story in his Yuddha Kandam as Vibhishana's warning to Ravana. When the king struck the pillar, the red-eyed lion laughed (Kamba Ramayanam, Iraniyan vathai padalam 127); Prahlada danced, wept and sang for joy (128); the pillar split and the lion grew to fill the eight directions (130); and at dusk, at the threshold of his golden palace, the Lord tore open the demon's chest (153). The Alvars sing the same scene, the Lord coming out of the pillar and tearing Hiranya's chest (Periya Thirumozhi 1.10.5, 3.10.4; Nanmugan Thiruvandhadhi 18).

Arunagiri recalls it in ten songs. Song 545 (Perainagar) praises the angry lion Narasingan who rose from inside the great golden pillar of the hall; song 150 (Palani) the lion who once took Hiranya's life; song 526 (Thiruvengadam) the man-lion. Murugan is the nephew of this Lord.
