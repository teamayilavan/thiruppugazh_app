On a day when the cowherd boys of Vraja had taken their calves to a pond to drink, and had drunk there themselves, they saw at the water's edge a huge body like a mountain peak broken off by a thunderbolt (Bhagavata Purana 10.11.46–47). It was the demon Baka, who had taken the form of an enormous crane with a very sharp beak, and he at once swallowed Krishna (10.11.48). Balarama and the other boys were struck senseless with fear, like bodies from which the life had gone (10.11.49).

Krishna burned the root of the demon's throat like fire, and the crane threw him out unhurt. When the demon came at him again with his beak, Krishna seized its two halves in his hands and split it apart as easily as a child splits a blade of grass (10.11.50–51). The gods showered flowers and sounded drums and conches; the boys, relieved, embraced him, gathered the calves and went back to Vraja to tell what had happened (10.11.52–53).

The story is one of a series in the tenth book of the Bhagavata in which demons come to Vraja in disguise and are overcome by the child Krishna in the middle of his play. The crane is the most unsettling of them, because it does not attack openly: it stands still at the water's edge, looking like part of the landscape, until it strikes.

The Alvars remember the deed in a phrase. Andal calls Krishna the one "who split the beak of the bird" and in the same line the one who plucked off the heads of the wicked Ravana (Tiruppavai 13), and Peyalvar counts the tearing of the crane's mouth among the Lord's wonders (Periya Thiruvandhadhi 64).

In the Thiruppugazh the crane appears once, in song 626 (Kunrakkudi). There Arunagiri lists the messengers that Kamsa sent against the child: the ogress Putana, whom Krishna overcame, and with her the wide-mouthed crane. Murugan is praised as the nephew of the Lord who met them all, as Arunagiri so often praises him, through the deeds of Vishnu.
