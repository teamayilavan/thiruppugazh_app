Thiruneelakanta Yazhpanar was a minstrel of the panar community, a player of the yazh, the ancient Tamil harp. The Periya Puranam tells that he came to Sirkazhi with his wife Matanga Chulamani, a singer, to worship the feet of the child saint (Periya Puranam, Sambandar Puranam 131). Sambandar welcomed them gladly, took them into the temple and asked the minstrel to play there for the Lord (vv. 132–134). The minstrel tuned the instrument, found the right mode and played the hymns while his wife sang; the Brahmins of the town were astonished, and the poet says the gandharvas and vidyadharas in the sky took up the song (vv. 135–136). Afterwards the minstrel asked to be allowed to play all of Sambandar's hymns on his yazh and never to be parted from him, and the saint agreed (vv. 140–141). From then on the minstrel and his wife set the hymns to the yazh and sang them (v. 278), and he travelled with Sambandar from Sirkazhi (v. 281).

The crisis came at Dharmapuram, the birthplace of the minstrel's mother. His kinsmen came out to honour him and said that the music of the hymns spread through the world because he set it on his instrument (vv. 444–445). Trembling, the minstrel bowed at the saint's feet and asked him to sing a hymn whose music the yazh could not contain, so that people would know the truth (v. 446). Sambandar sang the hymn beginning "Mathar Madappidi" (v. 447). When the minstrel tried to play it, its music would not go onto the strings (v. 448). In fear and sorrow he decided that the fault lay in his touching the instrument, and raised the yazh to break it (vv. 449–450). The saint stopped him and took the instrument, saying that the greatness of the grace given by Shiva could not be contained in such an instrument, and that it was enough to play the hymns as far as the yazh allowed (vv. 450–452). The minstrel received the yazh back with folded hands and placed it on his head (v. 452).

The film keeps the minstrel's motive as the Puranam gives it: not anger, but fear that his instrument had been given the credit due to the hymns. The heavenly musicians and the staging of the scenes are drawn as the verses suggest; the expressions are added for the screen.

The Thiruppugazh mentions the minstrel in one song. Song 354, sung at Thiruvanaikka, names Yazhpanar as the companion of Sambandar, the saint whom Arunagiri elsewhere identifies with Murugan himself.
