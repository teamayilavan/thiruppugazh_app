Rama and Sugriva sealed their alliance in the hills of Kishkindha, and Sugriva went to challenge his elder brother Vali, who had driven him out and taken his wife. In the first fight Rama did not loose his arrow, because he could not tell the two brothers apart, and Sugriva was beaten. Rama then had a flowering gaja creeper hung around Sugriva's neck so that he could be recognised in the fight (Valmiki Ramayana, Kishkindha Kanda 12.39–41), and Sugriva challenged Vali again. Valmiki tells that Vali's wife Tara, who had heard of Rama's alliance with Sugriva, warned her husband not to go out, but Vali would not refuse a challenge from his younger brother.

The brothers fought furiously. When Sugriva began to weaken, Rama, watching from cover, drew his bow and released an arrow that struck Vali in the chest, and Vali fell (Kishkindha 16.32–36). Valmiki goes on to give Vali's reproach, that Rama had struck him while he was fighting another and did not see him, and Rama's answer; then Vali's last words, in which he entrusts his son Angada to Sugriva. The question raised here has been discussed by commentators and readers for centuries, and the film does not try to settle it.

Kamban narrows the moment to three verses. Rama fitted the arrow, drew the string to his shoulder and loosed it (Kamba Ramayanam, Vali Vadhai Padalam 65); the arrow pierced the chest of the one who held the strength of the whole earth (66); and Vali fell as if Mount Meru had been torn up by its roots (67).

Among the Azhwars, Kulasekhara Azhwar sets Vali's death in the sequence of Rama's grief for Sita, his alliance with Sugriva and the burning of Lanka (Perumal Thirumozhi 10.6), and Periyazhwar includes it in his retelling of the whole story in a verse on Srirangam (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 4.9.1).

Arunagiri recalls Vali's death in seven songs, always in passing, as one of the deeds of the Lord whose nephew Murugan is. In song 113, at Palani, he speaks of the strong-armed Achyuta who loosed the arrow; in song 437, at Thiruvannamalai, of the one who gave Tara to Sugriva; in song 731, at Thiruvamathur, of the single arrow that brought down the seven trees with the angry Vali; and in song 840, at Vedaranyam, of the one who pierced the row of trees and Vali's chest. He does not dwell on the question of how Vali fell; for him the arrow is a sign of the Lord's power to help the one who has taken refuge.
