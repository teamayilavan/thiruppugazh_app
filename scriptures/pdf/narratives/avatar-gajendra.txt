In the Ocean of Milk there rose a great mountain, and on its slopes lived Gajendra, the king of the elephants, with his mates and his herd. Once, as he was bathing in a lake with his companions, a crocodile that was angry with him seized his leg. Gajendra struggled with all his strength; his wives wept, and the other elephants tried to drag him free from behind, but the crocodile was too strong for them (Bhagavata Purana 8.2.27–28).

For a thousand years the elephant and the crocodile pulled one another in and out of the water, and even the gods looked on in wonder. In the water the crocodile grew stronger and the elephant weaker (8.2.29–30). When Gajendra saw that no strength of his own could save him, he remembered a prayer he had learnt in a former life and offered it, speaking of the Supreme without naming any one god. For that reason Brahma, Shiva, Indra and the rest did not come; but Hari, the Lord who dwells in all, did. He appeared in the sky seated on Garuda, with his discus in hand (8.3.30–31). Gajendra, in his pain, raised a lotus in his trunk and bowed. Hari came down from Garuda, drew the elephant and the crocodile out of the water together, and killed the crocodile (8.3.32–33).

The Bhagavata then explains who the two creatures had been. Gajendra had been Indradyumna, a king of the Pandya country in the Tamil south, who retired to the Malaya hills to worship in silence. When the sage Agastya came to his hermitage and the king, deep in meditation, did not receive him, Agastya cursed him to take the dull body of an elephant (8.4.7–10). The crocodile had been the gandharva Huhu, who had pulled the leg of the sage Devala while bathing and was cursed by him to become a crocodile (8.4.3). Both were set free, and Gajendra was given a form like Vishnu's own (8.4.13).

The Alvars of the Tamil country sang the story often. Periyazhwar tells of the elephant seized by the crocodile and the Lord rushing to him on Garuda, and of the crocodile cut by the discus (Periyazhwar Thirumozhi 2.1.9 and 2.10.8); Nammazhwar asks what use are those who, having heard how the Lord saved the elephant, stay unmoved (Thiruvaimozhi 3.5.1). The discus that kills the crocodile is the Tamil tradition's own vivid detail, clearer than in the Sanskrit passage.

Arunagiri refers to the story in more than twenty songs, almost always in a single line. He calls Murugan the nephew of the Lord who answered the elephant's cry of "Mulame!" (the Tamil form of Gajendra's call to the first cause) and who destroyed the crocodile with his discus.
